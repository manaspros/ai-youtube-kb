> A buyer at a hedge fund explains why most AI startup pilots fail: value, security, reliability and legal gaps, not model quality. He argues most AI-native work is unglamorous data, architecture and change management.

## Summary
Brian Lewis, who works on product at Millennium (a hedge fund), speaks from the buyer side about which AI startups win enterprise contracts. He describes a funnel where roughly 5% of demo calls end in a signed contract, which he says matches industry benchmarks. He lists what he sees break deals: efficacy and commercial problems first, then security, reliability and legal. His checklist covers zero data retention or customer-managed keys that actually work, bring-your-own gateway, role-based access tied to directory groups and configurable via API, audit logs on config changes, staged rollouts, real SLAs, and clear sub-processor and indemnification terms. He argues that AI mostly acts as a flashlight on existing problems, and that about 60% of becoming AI-native is non-AI work such as data hygiene, clean architecture and change management (an estimate he calls unscientific). He ends with the point that agents inherit an organization's entitlement and process problems, and that a startup which meets a very high enterprise bar will likely meet most other buyers' bars. The talk is generic and has no technical implementation detail, but the checklist is concrete.

## Key ideas
- **The gap is not model capability. He argues most of the value current models could deliver is still unrealized inside enterprises.** — His stated hypothesis is that at current model intelligence most available value is left on the table, and the reasons are organizational and procurement-side. He frames the topic as the seller side (models and products) versus the buyer side (systems and the enterprise). ([1:21](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=81))
- **Enterprise AI sales is a steep funnel. About 5% of demo calls turn into signed contracts.** — From 10 to 15 candidate startups per pain point he takes 2 to 3 demos, which yields 0 or 1 pilots, and about one in four pilots becomes a contract. He says this tracks industry benchmarks he did not know existed. ([2:30](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=150))
- **Most deals die on value and commercial fit before security, reliability or legal even come up.** — He attributes about 40% of failures to efficacy and commercial issues, then security, reliability and legal. Named failure modes include vaporware that the buyer's platform team could rebuild in about six weeks, upside-down pricing, and features the buyer already declined being pitched again. He recommends the buyer, not the vendor, define the success criteria. ([3:59](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=239))
- **Pilot windows are shrinking fast.** — He says pilots that used to run about six months dropped to about three months and now may run around two weeks. ([5:29](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=329))
- **Reliability is a control-plane problem. Admin actions need an API, an audit trail and controlled rollout.** — His bar is that every admin setting is available via API, config changes appear in audit logs, the buyer controls rollout timing, and there are real SLAs, a reachable support engineer and a status page. Failure examples include a forced relaunch-to-update reaching about 3,000 users with no way to track what broke, and a core API down for hours during a trading day. ([9:36](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=576))
- **Legal and data-retention claims need to be verified in practice. Marketing terms are not enough.** — He describes vendors claiming zero data retention that still retained buyer data, which the buyer only discovered when the vendor referenced data it should not have had. He also flags features shipped as beta with permissive retention clauses, sub-processors hidden on support pages instead of in the contract, and the need for IP indemnification with reasonable liability caps. ([10:57](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=657))
- **About 60% of becoming AI-native is non-AI work, and AI acts as a flashlight that exposes existing problems.** — He says legacy architecture, data hygiene, integration, enablement and change management are the bulk of the work. He notes a new frontier model arrives about every 11 days while enterprise architectures may be a decade old, and ERP migrations from five years ago are still running. His 40/60 split is self-described as unscientific. ([13:15](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=795))
- **Agents inherit your foundations. Broken entitlements and rogue processes get amplified.** — He says the entitlement model needs a new paradigm before agents are deployed, because agents will act at much greater speed and scope than people. Other lessons: integrations must work across platforms, knowledge should be centralized and easily consumable so agents can be thin and the substrate smart, and some companies need a separate experimentation ecosystem. ([14:39](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=879))
- **A startup that meets a very high enterprise bar will likely clear most other buyers' bars.** — Millennium has about 8,000 people and tight security, compliance and regulatory requirements, which he says makes it one of the highest bars. On the buyer side, he says auth, entitlements, governance and audit logging deserve most of the investment. ([17:23](https://youtu.be/7A65O-0lvKE?t=1043))

## Techniques
- **Enterprise-readiness intake checklist for vendors** — Screen AI vendors on: zero data retention, or customer-managed keys verified to keep the product working; bring-your-own gateway routing; role-based access tied to directory or entitlement groups and configurable via API; scoped integrations with no default read-write-all; beta features off by default; a security architecture diagram before pilot; a named support path; and sub-processor and indemnification terms in the contract. *When:* Before a pilot or contract with any AI vendor, or when deciding whether a startup can pass procurement.
- **Verify retention claims in a pilot, not on paper** — Ask for retention behavior per feature, including beta features. Keep watching for vendor-side signs that data was kept longer than the contract allows, and test the claim with the vendor's own telemetry where possible. *When:* When a vendor says it has zero data retention or uses customer-managed keys.
- **Gate features by group and by release** — Deploy new features off by default and turn them on per entitlement group. Control the rollout timing yourself and avoid forced auto-updates that move a whole organization at once. *When:* Designing a multi-team AI tool or gateway that will be used across an enterprise.
- **Track documentation and terms as versioned artifacts** — Keep support pages, terms and sub-processor lists under version control so changes after signing are visible. Watch for risk language that appears only on a website and not in the contract. *When:* Running vendor risk management for SaaS or AI tools that update often.
- **Set buyer-written success criteria and a 90-day plan that deploys into the buyer's own cloud** — The buyer defines what success looks like, not the vendor, and the plan deploys into the buyer's infrastructure with weekly check-ins during the pilot. *When:* Structuring an enterprise pilot.
- **Build-versus-buy check on vendor pitches** — If the internal platform team could rebuild the pitched feature in about six weeks, treat the vendor as a candidate for buying only when the vendor adds real value on top. *When:* Evaluating a point-solution AI startup against an internal build option.
- **Breach-response question as a red flag test** — Ask the vendor what they will do if there is a breach. An answer like 'we have not had one yet' with no runbook signals a gap. *When:* Security review of an AI vendor during evaluation.

## Hot takes
- Most AI startup pitches are things an internal platform team could rebuild in about six weeks.
- Vendors promising zero data retention often still retain data, and beta features are used to slip in permissive retention clauses.
- About 60% of becoming AI-native is unglamorous non-AI work, and AI models 'can't fix your legacy architecture'.
- Agents will amplify whatever entitlement and process problems an organization already has, by roughly 100x.
- A startup that clears a very high enterprise bar will likely clear most other buyers' bars.
