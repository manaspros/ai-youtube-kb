> A news explainer on how a Roblox cheat download on a Context.ai employee's laptop led to a breach of Vercel, with the lesson that over-permissioned OAuth tokens and non-sensitive env vars made the blast radius large.

## Summary
The video walks through a reported Vercel breach, attributed to a chain that starts with a Roblox cheat. A Vercel employee had signed up for Context.ai's legacy AI Office Suite with a corporate Google Workspace account and granted it full Drive and Gmail access. A privileged Context.ai employee then downloaded a Roblox autofarm script onto a company laptop, which carried an infostealer (Luma Stealer) that harvested session cookies and credentials. The attackers used those to get into Context.ai's AWS environment, where they found a database of OAuth tokens for users of the legacy product, including the Vercel employee's. With that token they took over the Google Workspace account without a password or MFA prompt and reached internal Vercel systems, including a backend that could read non-sensitive environment variables. The speaker says the attackers later demanded $2 million on a breach forum on April 19, and that Vercel confirmed Next.js and Turbopack were unaffected and made sensitive the default for new env vars. The closing advice is to treat non-sensitive env vars as compromised, rotate them at the source, and audit Google Workspace OAuth grants for the Context.ai app. Most of the material is secondhand reporting, so the claims are unverified.

## Key ideas
- **Entry point was an over-permissioned third-party AI tool** — A Vercel employee signed up for Context.ai's legacy AI Office Suite with a corporate Google Workspace account and granted it full Drive and Gmail access. The speaker calls the broad grant reckless, though Context.ai itself is described as legitimate. ([0:44](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=44))
- **The real starting point was a privileged vendor employee's game cheat** — A Context.ai employee on a company laptop downloaded a Roblox autofarm script. It carried the Luma Stealer infostealer, which took live session cookies and corporate credentials, including Google Workspace logins and keys for Supabase, Datadog and AuthKit. ([1:49](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=109))
- **Stolen OAuth tokens enable pivoting without passwords or MFA** — Attackers used the stolen credentials to get into Context.ai's AWS environment and found a database of OAuth tokens for legacy product users. With the Vercel employee's token they took over that Google Workspace account without a password or MFA prompt, which gave access to Linear and a backend that could read non-sensitive env vars. ([1:49](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=109))
- **Env vars were non-sensitive by default, so exposure was broad** — Vercel env vars needed a manual checkbox to be marked sensitive. Sensitive values were encrypted and masked from internal systems, while the default was non-sensitive and readable in plain text internally. ([1:49](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=109))
- **Attacker claims and an unresolved attribution** — On April 19 the attackers posted on breach forums demanding $2 million. They claimed source code, npm and GitHub tokens and employee records, and posted a screenshot of an internal dashboard. Members of the Shiny Hunters group denied involvement, so this may be an affiliate or an impostor. ([3:07](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=187))
- **Vendor response: scope confirmation and a safer default** — Vercel confirmed core open source projects such as Next.js and Turbopack were not affected. All new env vars now default to sensitive. ([3:07](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=187))
- **Remediation means rotating secrets at the source** — Assume every non-sensitive env var was exposed and rotate those keys at their origin, since deleting the project or leaving the platform does not help. Companies should audit authorized OAuth apps in Google Workspace for the compromised Context.ai app. ([3:07](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=187))
- **The moral: a chain of small failures compromised a large platform** — The speaker summarizes the incident as one over-permissioned AI tool plus one employee's game cheat being enough to compromise a major infrastructure platform. The advice is not to install game hacks or untrusted software on work laptops. ([4:16](https://youtu.be/nnzH0eaAFa8?t=256))

## Techniques
- **Audit OAuth grants for third-party apps** — In Google Workspace, review the list of authorized third-party OAuth apps and look for the compromised Context.ai app. Revoke it and rotate any credentials it could have reached. The speaker points to this as the first check for companies worried about exposure. *When:* After a vendor breach or a suspected token theft from an AI or SaaS tool connected to corporate accounts.
- **Rotate secrets at the source after a platform compromise** — Treat every env var that was not marked sensitive as exposed. Rotate each key at its origin (the upstream provider), not just in the hosting dashboard. Deleting the project or moving off the platform does not invalidate the keys. *When:* When a hosting platform reports unauthorized access to internal systems that could read configuration values.
- **Mark env vars as sensitive for hosted platforms** — On platforms that offer it, check the sensitive option when creating each env var. Sensitive values are encrypted and masked from internal systems. Vercel now defaults new vars to sensitive, but existing vars and other platforms may still default to plain. *When:* Whenever a secret is stored in a third-party hosting platform's env config.
- **Least-privilege grants for AI tools on corporate accounts** — Do not give an AI productivity tool full Drive or Gmail access through a corporate Google account. Grant only the scopes the tool needs, and keep experimental tools off accounts that hold company data. *When:* Before a team member signs up for or connects a new third-party AI tool.
- **Treat OAuth tokens as credentials** — Model an OAuth refresh or access token as equivalent to a password, since it can grant access without a password or MFA prompt. Consider what a token database at a vendor would expose and how far one stolen token can reach. *When:* When designing or reviewing integrations that hold third-party OAuth tokens for many users.
