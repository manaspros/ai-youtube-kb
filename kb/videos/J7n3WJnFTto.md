> Long Lake's founding partner explains why buying services businesses and then deploying AI inside them beats selling AI agents from outside, using a J-curve and a services-economy margin argument.

## Summary
Prateek Naidoo of Long Lake (an applied-AI holding company) argues that AI vendors selling agents from the outside hit distribution, workflow-depth, and value-capture walls that are hard to clear. His alternative is to acquire mature services businesses and rebuild them around AI, so the vendor owns the outcome and the P&L impact. He frames services economics as roughly 80 cents of delivery and overhead per incremental dollar, and argues AI can give operating leverage so labor no longer scales linearly with revenue. He describes a J-curve from AI investment through capacity unlock to P&L impact, and a flywheel where live operations generate data that improves agents. The talk is a company pitch for Long Lake, with self-reported figures ($7.5B raised, 40+ partnered businesses, 55 staff, about 5,000 deals a year). It contains no technical content on agents, models or infrastructure.

## Key ideas
- **Selling AI agents from outside a company hits repeated walls: security review, integration with old systems, team adoption, and unclear revenue models (seats vs tokens).** — The speaker says the walls are organizational rather than model-related, since models are already capable. Adoption ownership between vendor and client is left open as a question. ([0:08](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=8))
- **Value created by an outside vendor often shows up in the client's P&L, not the vendor's, which makes value capture hard.** — Example: a $1M/yr contract that generates about $5M/yr EBITDA impact. Applying a conservative 10x multiple gives roughly $50M of enterprise value for the client, not the vendor. ([4:26](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=266))
- **Owning the business instead of selling into it solves access, data, incentive and trust problems together.** — Ownership gives direct access to veterans who hold tacit knowledge, a live environment with real edge cases as a feedback signal, incentive alignment with the business, and an existing brand and customer base. ([7:15](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=435))
- **Services businesses have a structural margin problem: roughly 80 cents of each incremental dollar goes to delivery and overhead, against about 20 cents of margin.** — Software keeps about 80 cents of each new dollar. AI can add operating leverage so labor cost no longer scales directly with revenue. ([10:19](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=619))
- **Long Lake's screening criteria for acquisitions are a trusted customer base with end-to-end outcomes, real human judgment in the service, and regulatory accountability.** — The speaker says intelligence is getting cheap, so the expensive things are the ones where the firm owns the outcome. Examples given: handling an urgent leak end to end, a complicated tax decision, or a travel disruption. ([13:04](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=784))
- **The business case follows a J-curve: investment in platform and workflow capture, then capacity unlock, then P&L impact once the capacity is redirected.** — Capacity gains such as time saved and fewer hours for the same output are not the end point. Real impact requires redirecting capacity into revenue per employee, NPS and service quality. ([15:02](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=902))
- **Ownership creates a compounding flywheel: agents and staff act in live operations, that produces outcome data, and the data improves the next deployment.** — The speaker says the playbook compounds across acquired businesses, giving faster time to deploy agents and faster time to P&L impact. ([8:49](https://youtu.be/J7n3WJnFTto?t=529))

## Techniques
- **Outcome-ownership screening filter for AI deployment targets** — Evaluate candidate businesses on three questions: does a customer base trust them with end-to-end outcomes, is there real human judgment in the service, and are there regulatory accountability requirements. Keep a large funnel and apply a tight filter. *When:* Choosing which services businesses to automate or acquire, rather than selling one tool into many companies.
- **Value-capture math for outside-in AI vendors** — Estimate client EBITDA impact from the agent, then apply a conservative multiple (the speaker uses 10x) to show the enterprise value created. Use it to see how much value sits in the client's P&L instead of the vendor's. *When:* Pricing an enterprise AI deal or deciding between seat, token and outcome pricing.
- **J-curve tracking for AI deployment** — Track three phases: investment (platform build, workflow capture, team time), capacity unlock (time saved, usage growth, same output in fewer hours), and P&L impact (revenue per employee, NPS, service quality). *When:* Measuring whether an AI rollout is delivering business value beyond usage metrics.

## Hot takes
- AI deployment is hard because of organizational complexity, not model quality, and vendors must embed inside the company to succeed.
- Owning the outcome, not the tool, is the more valuable position for AI companies, and services businesses are a larger opportunity than software.
- AI can turn labor-linked services economics into software-like economics through operating leverage.
