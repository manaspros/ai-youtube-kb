> Jump Trading's compute leads explain how GPU capacity trades bilaterally over chats, why cheaper tokens still raise compute demand, and how prepayments and financing gate startup capacity.

## Summary
Two compute-trading leads at Jump Trading, a proprietary trading firm, describe the GPU compute market as an unlit, "dark pool" style market. Most physical compute is sold in long bilateral block contracts (MSA, SLA and order form) negotiated over Slack, Telegram and similar chats rather than on an exchange, with the chip SKU dominating price talk. They argue that falling token prices do not reduce compute spend because of Jevons paradox, and they trace the macro chain from chips and power to grid, buildings and capital. The core of the talk is the financing gap: hyperscalers and frontier labs can make long commitments, while mid-market startups face large upfront prepayments that can consume a funding round. They walk through build-vs-rent economics by utilization, the roles of brokers, resellers, indexes and planned futures, and why every deal is bespoke because terms like "memory" are ambiguous. They close with brief policy context on export controls and open-weight policy, and a call for standard capacity contracts and balance-sheet players to bridge the middle market.

## Key ideas
- **Physical compute trades as private, bilateral block contracts, not on a public exchange.** — Deals are multi-year commercial agreements (MSA, SLA, cluster-specific order form), negotiated over chat channels and brokers. Pricing talk centers on the chip SKU (H100, H200, B300), while the actual negotiation happens out of public view. ([0:08](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=8))
- **Jevons paradox explains why compute prices stay firm while token prices fall.** — Cheaper tokens unlock more usage, so demand for compute rises. The speaker separates three quantities: the price of intelligence, the number of tasks people run, and the hardware needed to serve them. Capacity still has to be built and paid for. ([2:39](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=159))
- **The macro stack starts with chips and power, and capital is the binding constraint.** — Chips, power, buildings, grid connections and capital must all line up before any usable GPU-hour exists. The speaker cites an IEA April update projecting global data center electricity rising from 485 TWh (2025) to 950 TWh (2030). ([5:18](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=318))
- **Mid-market startups cannot make the long commitments that hyperscalers and labs can.** — Contracts of $200M+ over three years need large prepayments. A quoted 15-25% median prepayment on a $100M TCV cluster can absorb a startup's whole equity round. Lenders underwrite contracted revenue, not the company's own balance sheet. ([7:56](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=476))
- **Capacity planning is a step function gated by equity rounds, and on-demand can bridge gaps.** — Each new cluster requires a fresh prepayment tied to funding. Between rounds a company either turns away customers or buys on-demand capacity to cover the shortfall. The choice depends on whether the financed prepayment yield beats on-demand pricing at that time. ([9:25](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=565))
- **Build-vs-rent depends on utilization pattern, and compute procurement sets startup runway.** — Ownership should cost more than renting at the rates a buyer uses. Bursty, variable utilization can favor owning outright, while off-take contracts approach the cost of building and financing one's own cluster. Access terms such as bare-metal root access and topology control affect pricing. Procurement terms also shape a portfolio company's runway and therefore VC returns. ([10:47](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=647))
- **A growing set of market roles is forming: indexes, venues, resellers, brokers, flex contracts and lenders.** — Price discovery comes from indexes and benchmarks, with futures planned on them. Brokers source bilateral quotes and carry the risk of operating a cluster for a buyer. Buyers want flexible three-to-five-year contracts that allow upgrades or resale. Equipment lenders and specialist credit providers fill out the picture. ([13:12](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=792))
- **Every deal is bespoke because the same term can mean different things.** — Spec sheets, bills of materials and delivery may not match, and lead times are long. Terms like memory (VRAM, CPU memory or storage) are ambiguous. The speaker suggests separating the fungible SKU from cluster-specific terms. Public on-demand H100 prices serve only as reference points. ([15:49](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=949))
- **Financial hedges and new structures each solve only part of the risk.** — A hedge can reference a price but cannot deliver a cluster now or guarantee utilization. Locking capacity for years creates idle-hour exposure. Reselling spare capacity helps only if someone buys the hours. The useful structure starts from the specific risk a business must manage. ([17:17](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=1037))
- **Export controls and open-weight policy shape the market.** — The speaker cites a January move by the US Commerce Department that shifted qualifying H200-class license reviews for China and Macau to case-by-case review with conditions. The US AI Action Plan is cited as encouraging open-weight models and better compute financial markets. ([18:15](https://youtu.be/GCse4OMzY8U?t=1095))

## Techniques
- **Prepayment step-function capacity model** — Plot contracted capacity against demand over time. Take the equity raised, subtract the prepayment percentage on each cluster (the talk's example: raise $40M, $100M three-year contract, 30% prepay = $30M, leaving about $10M for operations), and find where demand exceeds contracted capacity. *When:* Planning how much GPU capacity a company can commit to before its next funding round, or deciding whether to finance a prepayment.
- **On-demand bridge versus financed prepayment comparison** — For a capacity gap, compare the yield on financing the prepayment with the cost of buying the same hours on demand at that time. Turn away customers only if neither option pencils out. *When:* Deciding how to serve demand above committed capacity between funding rounds.
- **Build-vs-rent by utilization curve** — Chart ownership cost (capex, financing, operations) against rental or off-take cost across utilization levels. Rent when utilization is variable or low. Own outright when sustained use stays above the crossover point. Count the value of physical access, confidential compute and topology control. *When:* Choosing between owning hardware, co-locating, or signing off-take and rental contracts for GPU capacity.
- **Decompose a quote into fungible and non-fungible parts** — Separate the standardized parts (SKU, such as H100 or B300) from cluster-specific terms (memory type, storage, delivery, BOM substitutions, lead times). Define each ambiguous term explicitly before comparing quotes. *When:* Comparing bilateral compute quotes or drafting a standard capacity contract with common specs and definitions.
- **Risk-first structure selection** — Name the specific risk the business carries (price exposure, idle hours, upgrade timing, delivery timing) before choosing among long contracts, flex or upgrade rights, resale rights, futures or prepayment facilities. *When:* Designing a compute procurement or hedging strategy for a company that buys or sells GPU capacity.

## Hot takes
- Compute procurement, not model choice, may decide a startup's runway, and the middle market is squeezed between long hyperscaler commitments and expensive short-term on-demand capacity.
- Jevons paradox is offered as the explanation for why token prices fall while compute prices hold. It is argued qualitatively, with no measured usage data in the talk.
- The a16z graph, CoreWeave prepayment figure and IEA projection are cited secondhand. None are verified in the talk.
- Thin on engineering: this is market structure and financing from a trading firm's vantage point, and it closes with a recruiting-style call to action ("let's build this together"). No sponsored segment is visible.
