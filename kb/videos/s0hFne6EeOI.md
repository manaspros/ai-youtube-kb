> Kent C. Dodds on product engineering: turn feature requests into jobs-to-be-done, classify them with the Kano model, and build the right thing before building it well.

## Summary
Kent C. Dodds (Part 2 of a product engineering series) walks through a worked example of a conference app request, "facial recognition," and asks "why" repeatedly until it becomes a job-to-be-done statement. He argues a feature request is not the job, so product engineers must apply judgment before implementing anything, and that the job lens gives them better questions about functional, social and emotional constraints. For validation before metrics exist he recommends the Mom Test and quick prototypes, and he notes that users are directionally right but not always right. The second half uses the Kano model (basic, performance, delighter, indifferent, reverse) to prioritise a food delivery app's requests and explains that features only move down the scale over time, so basics must be maintained. He closes with the thesis that once agents level implementation, the differentiator is building the right thing. Much of the content is general product thinking rather than AI-specific, and the AI references are light.

## Key ideas
- **A feature request is not the job. Ask 'why' repeatedly to build a problem tree until you reach the underlying job to be done.** — The speaker asks why the facial recognition feature matters. The answer moves from 'it's slow' to 'get people into the room and know who came in'. He says engineers should keep asking why and then stop at the root problem before choosing a solution. ([1:41](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=101))
- **Requests need product judgment. Do not pipe them straight into implementation, and do not let an agent implement them automatically.** — Every feature adds complexity that must be maintained. He warns against an app that sends each user request to an agent for automatic implementation, because the result has no vision or taste. ([7:56](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=476))
- **Write a job statement: 'When [situation], help me make progress so I can [outcome]'. Then break it into functional, social and emotional dimensions.** — The example statement is about getting into a workshop quickly without missing the first 20 minutes. Each dimension maps to technical decisions: functional asks whether it does the thing, social asks who is involved, and emotional asks how the user feels, including whether they feel embarrassed or uneasy. ([9:25](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=565))
- **Success is repeated use, not only the sale. The customer is the buyer (here, the conference), not the end user.** — The big hire is the purchase. The little hire is each repeated use, like each bite of a burger. Define success by repeated progress and ask whether the paying customer will use the product again. ([21:15](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=1275))
- **Before any metrics exist, validate the problem, build a rough prototype, and watch people use it. Ask about past behaviour, not hypothetical interest.** — Mom Test style questions such as 'when was the last time you had this problem and what did you do instead?' surface real pain. AI makes quick prototypes cheap. If nobody will try the prototype, that is a signal. ([24:04](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=1444))
- **The user is not always right, but is often directionally right. Users propose solutions, so ask about the underlying pain and check it with other users.** — One heavy user may not represent others. Confirm by asking the wider user base about the same pain. A request from a user who covers most of the revenue may justify building, while a request from one of many small users may not. ([25:20](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=1520))
- **Kano model: features sort into basic (must-have, over-building gives no gain), performance (more is better, linear), and delighter (unexpected, valuable when it is present).** — Indifferent features should be removed because they are dead weight to maintain. Reverse features are ones users hate, and engineers should push back on building them. The speaker says the axes have no units and the model is a guide. ([31:53](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=1913))
- **Features only move one way on the Kano scale. A delighter becomes a performance need, then a basic need. GPS moved from delighter in 2015 to basic now.** — Because of this drift, teams must keep checking competitors and re-sorting features. Shipping a delighter does not earn lasting advantage, and basics such as reliable email notifications must be kept complete. ([43:10](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=2590))
- **Foundations come first. Do not over-invest in delighters before basics are reliable, and give each category a different technical treatment.** — Basics need reliability, completeness and ownership. Performance needs measurable quality gradients with thresholds. Delighters are experiments that can run in a separate system and be merged back once their shape is clear. Indifferent features are removed. ([44:37](https://youtu.be/s0hFne6EeOI?t=2677))

## Techniques
- **Problem tree via repeated 'why'** — Ask why a request matters, then why that problem matters, going several levels deep. Stop when the problems are no longer big enough, and note where a different earlier choice would have led somewhere else. *When:* When a feature request arrives without a stated underlying problem, or before choosing any implementation.
- **Job statement template** — Write 'When [situation], help me make progress so I can [outcome]', optionally with 'without [pain]'. Then list functional, social and emotional dimensions and let them drive the technical decisions. *When:* To turn a request into a job that can be used to evaluate solutions, and to find solutions that serve several requests at once.
- **Little-hire tracking** — Measure repeated use of the product (for example, how often the paying customer returns), not only the first purchase or sign-up. *When:* When designing success metrics for B2B or institutional products where the buyer differs from the end user.
- **Mom Test validation** — Interview users about past behaviour and recent pain ('when was the last time you had this problem and what did you do?'), not about whether they would like a feature. Then prototype and watch them try it. *When:* Before building anything when there are no usage metrics yet, and when deciding whether one user's request reflects a broader need.
- **Kano classification for prioritisation** — Sort candidate features into basic, performance, delighter, indifferent or reverse. Set reliability requirements for basics, measurable thresholds for performance, and time-boxed experiments for delighters. Remove indifferent features and avoid building reverse ones. *When:* When a backlog of requests arrives and you must decide what to build, what to measure and what to remove.
- **Feedback aggregation channel** — Collect user complaints in one place, such as a Slack channel fed by a few trusted high-value customers and by social media threads. Use the patterns to find paper cuts and missed jobs. *When:* When you cannot read all user feedback directly, as in large products with millions of users.

## Hot takes
- Do not take feature requests and implement them directly. Stop and ask what job they serve.
- Once agents make implementation cheap, building the right thing is the most valuable skill engineers have.
- Engineers should push back on harmful feature ideas, because software engineers are the ones who build them.
- Basics cannot be over-delighted. Foundations and reliability come before experiments.
