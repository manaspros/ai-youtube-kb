> Critical walkthrough of JEV (TypeSafe AI), a model that returns only probabilities for typed decision questions instead of text; fast and cheap, but accuracy and calibration caveats are the real story.

## Summary
The narrator examines JEV, a TypeSafe AI model launched in September 2026 that produces no output text. Callers send program state plus typed questions (choice, score, or a Bernoulli yes/no probability) and get back a decision and a probability in roughly 70 to 500 ms, priced at $42 per billion input tokens with free output. The speed comes from skipping token-by-token generation and reading the probability distribution the model already computes. The narrator reports the vendor's 193.6x speed claim as real but measured against non-reasoning frontier models, and notes that accuracy agreement with reference answers (67.8% overall, 61.8% on invoices) trails GPT 5.6 Sol. He covers the vendor's published list of weaknesses (counting, dates, negation consistency), the difference between confidence and calibrated probability, a poker-solver test with 63% top-action match, and an open-weights reimplementation that got a 5.21x speedup on Qwen but a much lower quality score. His conclusion is to price the decision, not the demo, and to test thresholds on your own data before wiring it into a pipeline.

## Key ideas
- **JEV returns no text at all. Callers get back a decision plus a probability from three typed query kinds: choice (up to 255 options, full distribution), score (2 to 10 ordered levels), and Bernoulli (one probability that a statement is true).** — The API maps to match, sort, and if-statements in code. Latency is 70 to 500 ms, most calls near 100 ms. Pricing is $42 per billion input tokens with free output because no output tokens exist. ([1:16](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=76))
- **The speed comes from refusing to generate tokens. A yes/no answer from an LLM still costs one forward pass per output token, and JEV evaluates all questions in parallel against the same context, then returns the softmax distribution before it is discarded.** — The narrator argues the 193.6x speed figure is real but compares against non-reasoning frontier models run on the vendor's own workflows, and that the vendor's post admits possible bias. ([5:14](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=314))
- **The accuracy column is where the claims weaken. Across four workflows JEV agrees with the reference answer 67.8% of the time, versus 67.9% for GPT 5.6 Terra and 74.1% for GPT 5.6 Sol. On invoices it is 61.8% versus 79.1%.** — The reference answers are averages of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 outputs, so the benchmark measures agreement with two other frontier models, not ground truth. ([3:52](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=232))
- **Schema-forced output is not the same as the vendor's approach. The narrator relays the argument that forcing an LLM into a schema masks invalid tokens and papers over model confusion, so the model should error instead.** — An open reimplementation by Theo Lee wrapped Qwen and read logits directly. On an RTX 3090 it returned a decision in 1.023 s versus 5.332 s for the same model writing a JSON array (5.21x). A quality gap remained: 82.9% for JEV vs 58.8% for the reimplementation on MMLU-Pro. ([6:31](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=391))
- **The vendor's own 'jaggedness' page lists nine weaknesses: counting (error grows with size), reading dates as text, hex/RGB/binary, distractor-heavy state, double negatives, and inconsistent probabilities (P(x) and 1-P(not x) do not sum to one).** — The narrator praises the vendor for publishing this. Confidence is a concentration statistic, not probability; the docs say to set your own thresholds and test them. ([7:49](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=469))
- **A schema constrains the shape of the answer, not its truth. A cafeteria-hours question given only billing, technical, and sales categories returned sales at confidence 0.31: valid output, wrong answer.** — On state-injection, a fake system override in a support ticket left the classification unchanged and the flag at 3% in one attempt. The narrator calls this one test, not a security posture, though the docs say state is not treated as hostile by default. ([9:05](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=545))
- **A poker solver gives known-correct answers. On a solved flop with 0.59% exploitability, JEV shoved all 16 runs where the solver checks 100% of the time; across 30 spots it matched the top action 63% of the time. The author's complaint is that no evals were published.** — The narrator's advice is to evaluate on your own data before wiring a decision model into production. ([10:25](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=625))
- **Price the decision, not the demo. At 1,000 input tokens per call and a million calls a month, the cost is about $42 total. That makes many decisions previously skipped for cost economical, which is a Jevons argument.** — Rate limit 1,200 requests/min and 250,000 tokens/s. JEV is hosted and not open source, and no weights are downloadable. It can be reached via Vercel, Cloudflare, and OpenRouter. ([11:50](https://youtu.be/BGDd26FiOm4?t=710))

## Techniques
- **Probability gate instead of a generated yes/no** — Ask a Bernoulli-style question that returns P(statement is true) and branch on a threshold. Choose the threshold on your own labeled data, not on the confidence field. The narrator says confidence is not probability and the docs say to set thresholds yourself. *When:* High-volume binary decisions such as spam, routing, or guardrail checks where generating a full JSON answer costs more latency and money than the decision is worth.
- **Choice query with full distribution for routing** — Pass the candidate options (up to 255) and read back the whole probability distribution. Act on the top option only when its probability clears a threshold, and fall back otherwise. Keep the option list to valid, exhaustive categories, since the model will always choose from the list. *When:* Ticket triage, ranking, or tool selection where the set of valid answers is closed and known in advance.
- **Tool selection without a language model in the loop** — Send existing tool definitions plus state as the program state. The decision model returns which tool to call and with which argument, and ordinary code executes the call. Nothing is generated as text, so nothing is invented in the reply. Demo: a light-switch command in about 300 ms. *When:* Command or tool dispatch where the action set is fixed and the latency of a generative model is the bottleneck.
- **Per-tool-call relevance filter for Claude Code** — Score each tool call or tool result for relevance to the current task and drop the irrelevant ones before they enter the context. A session dropped from about 1M tokens to 86k, per a third-party demo the narrator cites. *When:* Long coding-agent sessions where tool output bloats context and costs tokens.
- **Adversarial state test before trusting the output** — Embed a fake system override in the state (for example, a support ticket telling the model to reroute and flip flags). Check whether the classification and flag values stay unchanged. Repeat with many attempts before relying on it. *When:* Any decision pipeline that reads untrusted user content in its state.
- **Evaluate against a solver or known-correct baseline** — Use a domain with mathematically known answers (the narrator used poker with a solver at 0.59% exploitability) to measure top-action match over random spots, rather than relying on agreement with other LLMs. *When:* Before choosing a decision model for any domain where a ground-truth oracle exists.
- **Local logit read of an open model for a single decision** — Wrap an open-weights model and read the logits for the candidate answer tokens directly, instead of having the model write a JSON array. Reported by Theo Lee on Qwen with an RTX 3090 (1.023 s vs 5.332 s for the same question). Steps beyond the logit read were not shown in the video. *When:* A single fixed decision that must run on your own hardware or offline, where a probability-only answer is enough.
- **Price a decision workload before adopting** — Estimate monthly cost as calls per month x input tokens per call x price per token. Example in the video: 1,000 tokens x 1M calls at $42/B = $42/month, with output costing nothing. *When:* Deciding whether a cheap per-decision model is worth replacing a rule, a small local classifier, or a frontier model call.

## Hot takes
- Speed is real, but the 193.6x comparison is against non-reasoning frontier models on the vendor's own workflows, which the vendor admits may be biased. Accuracy is the number nobody quoted.
- The accuracy reference is not ground truth. It is an average of two other frontier models, so agreement measures similarity to those models.
- Confidence is a concentration statistic, not probability, and the probability for a statement and its negation do not sum to one. A schema guarantees the shape of an answer, not its truth.
- Launch coverage (25 videos in 72 hours) repeated the 193.6x figure and skipped the accuracy and jaggedness caveats. The narrator's bottom line is to price the decision, not the demo.
- The open re-implementation shows the logit trick is reproducible in a weekend, so the moat is training quality (82.9% vs 58.8% on MMLU-Pro), not the architecture.
