> Kai Explains argues Oracle's Project Jupiter force majeure notice is not an exit signal; the real constraint on AI capacity is power and permits, not demand.

## Summary
The video walks through Oracle's September 24, 2026 force majeure notice on Project Jupiter, a 2.45 GW data center in New Mexico built for OpenAI. The host reads the notice as a delay-protection filing rather than an exit, because the lease is hell-or-high-water and Oracle keeps paying carry costs during any delay. The trigger was a 0.6-mile state trust land crossing on a 17-mile gas pipeline that feeds on-site fuel cells, which pushed the in-service date to February 1, 2027. The host then traces money across three layers (Blue Owl equity, about 20 banks with an $18B loan, and Oracle as tenant) and shows the loan was already trading down before the notice. The larger concern is $288B of unrecognized lease commitments against negative free cash flow and a backlog about half owed by OpenAI. The host concludes demand is accelerating and the bottleneck is electricity, and names four dates to watch. There is no engineering content; the value is a worked example of reading filings and cash flows against a headline.

## Key ideas
- **The force majeure notice is a delay-protection filing, not an exit** — The notice went to Stack Infrastructure (Blue Owl-owned campus owner), not OpenAI. It asks for the right to delay rent up to three years if power is late. Oracle still owes a carry cost covering full project interest plus a guaranteed return to Blue Owl investors. The host says the lease cannot be terminated, so the notice is evidence there is no exit. ([0:00](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=0))
- **The trigger was a 0.6-mile state trust land crossing on a 17-mile pipeline** — Jupiter makes its own power with Bloom Energy fuel cells running on natural gas, so the gas pipeline is on the critical path. A New Mexico land commissioner denied the crossing on March 20 and again on July 14, citing emissions, water risk and little benefit to the trust. Oracle got a federal route approved on BLM land under a 14-day emergency review, but in-service slipped to February 1, 2027. ([7:21](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=441))
- **Hell-or-high-water leases shift construction risk onto the tenant** — The host explains that the landlord borrows against the rent, so the tenant pays whether or not the building gets power, chips or a pipeline. This is the structure the host says every one of Oracle's long-dated leases shares. ([2:43](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=163))
- **Market pricing of the loan moved before the notice** — The $18B project loan was quoted at 89 to 91 cents on September 18, six days before the notice. Oracle's debt default insurance was about 2% a year in July, versus under 1% for Meta and Nvidia. The host argues the market had already priced the stress before the letter. ([6:11](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=371))
- **Cash flow is the uncomfortable part** — For the quarter ending August 31, revenue was $19.3B and capex $28.5B. About $11.4B of operating cash came from customer prepayments for capacity not yet built. Without that, free cash flow for the quarter is roughly minus $17B. ([11:22](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=682))
- **Off-balance-sheet lease commitments nearly tripled in a year** — Commitments reached $288B as of August 31, mostly data center leases of 15 to 19 years, expected to come online between this fall and 2029. A year earlier the figure was $99.8B. The host compares this to a roughly $400B market cap. ([9:59](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=599))
- **Demand is accelerating, so the constraint is supply, specifically power** — OpenAI's revenue run rate roughly doubled this year and Oracle cloud infrastructure revenue grew 121% last quarter. GE Vernova reported 116 GW of backlog and reservations against about 20 GW a year of production. Currents estimated 30 to 50% of large data center capacity due this year would be delayed rather than canceled. ([13:57](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=837))
- **The bear case has counterpoints the host takes seriously** — Force majeure notices are routine and often have 10-day filing windows, so filing may be timely paperwork. Work continues with over 3,600 workers on site, and one analyst estimates Jupiter is about 1% of capacity under construction. Oracle is rated BBB minus (investment grade), and the one-in-five default figure circulating online comes from credit insurance pricing that includes a fear premium. ([15:17](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=917))
- **Four dates are the signals to watch** — November 3 New Mexico election (the land commissioner is on the Democratic lieutenant governor ticket), November 23 air permit decision for the Jupiter power plant, February 1, 2027 pipeline in-service date, and Oracle's mid-December earnings call. ([16:32](https://youtu.be/mNozPwLvJIY?t=992))

## Techniques
- **Read the contract and filing before the headline** — Check what the notice actually asks for (here, delayed rent plus a carry cost) against what the headline claims (exit). Look for the clause that determines who bears risk, such as the termination language or the hell-or-high-water structure. *When:* When a company filing or press item is read as a distress signal and you need to tell a real exit from a routine delay-protection notice.
- **Trace cash flow by layer and strip out one-off inflows** — Split the capital stack into equity, debt and tenant layers, then compute each layer's return. Remove customer prepayments from operating cash flow to get the underlying operating cash, then subtract capex for free cash flow. *When:* When an operating cash flow number looks healthy but a large share comes from prepayments for capacity not yet delivered.
- **Compare market timestamps with news timestamps** — Check when a loan price or credit spread moved relative to the news event. If the move came first, the news was confirmation rather than the cause. *When:* When judging whether a news event is the cause of a market move or a late signal of stress that was already priced in.

## Hot takes
- Oracle is not exiting; the notice is a lawyer's footnote, and the bottleneck is electricity and permits rather than demand.
- The $288B lease book is the real risk, more than this one campus. Each lease has the same long, hard-to-exit structure and the same delay exposure.
- The 'one in five default' figure circulating online is mostly a fear premium in credit insurance pricing, not a neutral forecast.
