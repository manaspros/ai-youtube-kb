> Kai investigates "Stealth Ox Alpha", a free OpenRouter model with no stated developer, using tokenizer offsets, error strings and a leaked system prompt; he concludes the evidence points to GLM but is unproven.

## Summary
A free, anonymous model ("Stealth Ox Alpha") appeared on OpenRouter with a blank developer field, a null knowledge cutoff, and a single provider called Stealth, which the speaker says is OpenCode. The video walks through how people fingerprinted it: a tokenizer that differs from GLM 5.3 by exactly 75 tokens on every test text, identical error wording, German-style decimals, and a video token count that one tester matched and another did not. A hidden system prompt was extracted about 23 hours after launch, and the model leaked its identity with no jailbreak at all. The speaker lays out the compute and data-retention contradictions (OpenCode says zero retention, OpenRouter says prompts are retained by the provider) and says the GLM theory has a serious compute objection. The benchmark claims are weak (8/10 tasks, which is 49-94% at 95% confidence), and a single hands-on comparison showed the free model was much slower than a paid one. The conclusion sorts claims into confirmed, strongly suggested, and not proven, and advises against sending anything sensitive through the endpoint.

## Techniques
- **Tokenizer offset fingerprinting** — Send the same texts to the unknown endpoint and to candidate known models, covering English, German, Chinese, source code, emoji, Hindi, Arabic, C++ templates, base64, math symbols and 200 consecutive spaces. Compute token-count deltas. A delta that stays constant across all inputs suggests a shared tokenizer plus a fixed prefix. Run unrelated models as controls, since their deltas drift. *When:* Working out whether an opaque endpoint shares a lineage with a known model, before trusting its provider label.
- **Error-string matching** — Send a deliberately invalid parameter (the video uses an invalid reasoning setting) and compare the error code and wording with known models' responses. *When:* When the model's own answers can be instructed or faked but its serving-stack errors are harder to imitate.
- **Temperature-zero output comparison** — Set temperature to 0 and ask factual questions to the unknown and candidate models. Compare text, markdown habits and locale-specific formatting quirks, such as decimal commas inside LaTeX. *When:* As a secondary behavioral signal, since near-identical answers are weak evidence on their own.
- **Controlled video token test** — Send clips at different resolutions and frame rates, count the tokens each produces, and check frame sampling, resolution handling and audio handling. Repeat it because the video reports two testers disagreed. *When:* When a model claims multimodal input and you need to know whether its vision pipeline matches a known family's.
- **Report benchmark counts with intervals** — Report pass/fail counts as k/n with a confidence interval, not as a percentage. The video's example is 8/10, which is 49-94%. *When:* Any small eval run, such as a 10-task subset, before comparing it to other models.
- **Cost and time comparison on a fixed build task** — Run the same app-build task across models and record wall time, tokens, dollar cost and tokens per second, plus latency to first token. *When:* Choosing between a free and a paid model for agentic coding work.
- **Check retention claims at both the aggregator and the provider** — Compare the data-retention wording on the aggregator's model page with the provider's launch post. If they conflict, assume the stricter one applies and keep sensitive data off the endpoint. *When:* Routing traffic through a multi-provider gateway to a model with no named developer.

## Hot takes
- The stated retention terms conflict. OpenCode says zero data retention and OpenRouter says prompts are retained by the provider. The speaker's advice is to assume retention and keep sensitive data off the endpoint.
- The viral '80%' result was 8 of 10 tasks. The speaker says the sample size got dropped on the way around the internet and that the honest interval is 49-94%.
- The GLM theory is strong on fingerprints, but the speaker says the compute bill (6.5T tokens/day free at four nines) is a real objection to it. He does not resolve this.
- The Gemini theory rests on a one-word reply from a DeepMind engineer, which the speaker treats as anecdote, not evidence.
- The speaker explicitly refuses to name the builder ('Nobody does'). The video is careful and hedged, with no sponsor or hype framing.
