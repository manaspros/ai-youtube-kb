> Vinoo Ganesh on running forward-deployed engineering as a product strategy: go on site, solve small problems fast, define shared terms, and avoid shipping hacks that become permanent.

## Summary
Vinoo Ganesh, who built Palantir's Project Frontline rotation, argues that forward-deployed engineering (FDE) is a product function, not a go-to-market role. He tells the story of Palantir's 2013 Phoenix storage system, which was designed in isolation and failed on real bank data with blank dates defaulting to the epoch. He then describes a shipping-company engagement where a 47-page requirements doc and a three-month estimate were replaced by a Slack alert that took four hours, after asking what the dispatcher does on Monday morning. A data-quality engineer who resisted Parquet was watched on site, and a small viewer built overnight led to an approved migration and a pipeline that dropped from about 17 hours to about 2. The talk then covers defining shared vocabulary as an ontology, and a Groovy script that was never productized and had to be supported for years. He closes with calibration questions to ask before shipping a fix and the claim that FDEs should be judged by the product they ship.

## Key ideas
- **FDE is a product strategy role, not a go-to-market role.** — The speaker says an FDE is judged by how well they extend the product team, find opportunities, and generalize them into product solutions. He argues the role should not be treated as a sales or customer-success function. ([1:25](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=85))
- **Building in isolation without customer contact caused the Phoenix failure.** — The team built a bank data system without talking to customers. Blank dates defaulted to the epoch, and the retention design needed about 2.3 million key spaces. Cassandra's per-file-handle memory cost would have needed around 14 TB of RAM just to start. ([2:45](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=165))
- **Find the real problem before building anything.** — A 47-page requirements doc and a planned three-month build were replaced by one question about what the dispatcher does on Monday morning. The real need was a simple alert, which took four hours. Customers describe solutions; the FDE's job is to find the problem and what happens after it is solved. ([4:03](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=243))
- **If a fix is under a day of work, ship it and close the loop.** — For small problems the speaker says not to bring in PMs or expand the product vision. Whoever defines the problem and ships the fix ends up owning the system and the narrative around later solutions, which is how FDEs earn early trust. ([6:56](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=416))
- **Watch what users actually do on site instead of trusting what they say.** — A data-quality engineer opposed Parquet for a year. Watching her work showed she double-clicked CSVs on a Windows machine to spot-check them, and Parquet had no native viewer. The team built a viewer that night, and she approved the migration within two days. ([8:17](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=497))
- **Repeated actions, copy-paste between tools, tab switching and phone use mark where to look for opportunities.** — The speaker lists signals a user gives off during a workflow: a task done several times a day or week, moving data between tools, exasperated 'I have to' reactions, switching tabs, and reaching for a phone mid-task. The goal is to make the user's tomorrow different from their today. ([9:37](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=577))
- **Define the shared language and ontology, because users adopt the words you use.** — Sales, ops, finance and dev each use a different word for the same customer entity, and that causes broken integrations and pipelines. The speaker says organizations are built from nouns (entities) and verbs (operations), and the FDE should map overloaded terms, integration points and system boundaries and then canonicalize them in the product. ([13:10](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=790))
- **Ship fast, but treat every hack as permanent.** — A Groovy data-retention script written as a temporary fix ran for years at a roughly 100,000-person customer and was never productized. The speaker says 'this is just temporary' is the most dangerous phrase in engineering, and that FDEs should ship as if the code will run for 18 months. ([16:02](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=962))
- **Use calibration questions to decide what goes into the core product.** — Ask whether this will cause a 2 a.m. call in six months, who owns it after you leave the site, and whether it is mission-critical. Decide whether to fold the fix into the core offering or discard it, rather than letting customer goodwill drive the choice. ([18:32](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=1112))
- **Measure an FDE by the product they shipped, not by what they learned.** — The speaker's closing claim is that product leverage is the only thing that wins customers. He says the question is what the FDE shipped from a product perspective, and that early-stage companies should not use FDEs as a go-to-market extension. ([20:47](https://youtu.be/1OMHGsUZiqA?t=1247))

## Techniques
- **Three-question discovery before building** — Before writing code, ask what the customer is trying to accomplish, what happens after the problem is solved, and how they solve it today. Ask about the actual next action (for example, what they do Monday morning) rather than trusting the written requirements. *When:* At the start of any customer or internal engagement where a requirements doc or large estimate is on the table.
- **Ship-it threshold for small problems** — If a problem can be solved in under a day, build it directly and close the loop without a product review. Own the fix and use the resulting narrative to shape later work. *When:* When a customer has a narrow, well-defined pain point and a full product process would cost more than the fix.
- **On-site workflow observation** — Sit with the user and watch them complete the workflow end-to-end, including manual steps like downloading files, opening them and checking them. Build the missing tool that night if needed, rather than arguing about the design. *When:* When users resist a technical change (for example a file format or pipeline change) and their reasons are unclear.
- **Pain-signal scan** — Look for tasks repeated several times a day or week, copy-paste between tools, tab switching, and points where users pick up a phone or express exasperation. Treat each as a candidate problem to solve. *When:* When looking for product opportunities inside an existing user workflow.
- **Canonical glossary and ontology** — List terms that teams use differently (for example customer vs client vs billing entity vs org ID), map the translation layers at integration points, pick one canonical term, and codify it in the product so users adopt that vocabulary. *When:* When integrations break or data quality issues come from teams using the same word for different things.
- **Production-readiness gate for quick fixes** — Before shipping a temporary fix, ask whether it will page someone in six months, who owns it after handoff, and what happens when it breaks. If the answer is bad, productize it or discard it. Design as if it will run for 18 months. *When:* Before shipping any customer-specific script, workaround or hack.

## Hot takes
- Forward-deployed engineering is a product strategy, not a go-to-market function. Early-stage companies should not use FDEs as go-to-market extensions.
- 'This is just temporary' is the most dangerous phrase in engineering; every hack that solves a problem goes to production and lives on.
- Users don't just adopt your product, they adopt your language, so whoever defines the vocabulary becomes the foundation other tools sit on.
- Being badged on site is a data-mining permit; surveys cannot surface the problems that actually matter.
- Customers describe solutions, not problems.
