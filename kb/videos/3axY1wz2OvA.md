> Oliver Kennan (SemiAnalysis) argues GPU cost per useful token depends on the operator's serving software and ops discipline, not just the chip, and that cost positions are relative to competitors, which matters for compute futures and rental price hedges.

## Summary
SemiAnalysis research analyst Oliver Kennan argues that the same GPU can yield very different useful output depending on who operates it. He uses a B200 example from SemiAnalysis's public inference calculator: at the same 100 tok/s per-user target with about 8k input and 1k output tokens, one serving stack gives roughly 200 output tok/s and another roughly 300, so the buyer's rent buys about 50% more usable output. He frames intelligence as the commodity output, price per successful task as the North Star, and cost per acceptable token as the producer metric. He stresses that operating work (secure multi-tenant access, testing and rolling out serving updates, rollback, on-call) is a continuing cost that has to be justified by the extra output. He presents ClusterMax 3.0, which rates managed GPU clusters on provisioning, reliability, monitoring and security, and says ratings do not transfer to bare metal. He argues the cost position is relative: a producer that improves can still fall behind if competitors improve faster. He closes by saying rating operators is a building block for a credible compute futures market, while warning that a rental index does not measure operating quality and a hedge only works if the index tracks the rent actually paid. The talk is vendor-adjacent (SemiAnalysis sells research and ratings) but mostly conceptual, with one worked example and no reproducible method.

## Key ideas
- **Hardware is only potential. The operator, who picks and runs the serving software and keeps requests flowing, decides how much usable work the GPU delivers.** — Software and ops multiply the hardware and electricity inputs. Same chip, different stack, very different output. ([2:39](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=159))
- **Same B200 hardware at the same per-user speed target yields about 50% more output with a better serving stack.** — At a P50 target of 100 output tok/s per user, one stack gives about 200 output tok/s and another about 300 at P90, with ~8k input and ~1k output tokens. If the hourly bill is equal, rent per acceptable token is about 1/1.5 of the other. ([3:42](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=222))
- **Useful intelligence needs more than speed. It has to meet quality, latency, availability and security requirements.** — He cites a monitoring endpoint that leaked another customer's logs and metrics. The service was fast but failed on security, a gap that chip performance metrics do not capture. ([4:48](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=288))
- **Operating a deployment is ongoing work: test updates on the customer workload, check outputs under load, keep a rollback path, and own on-call.** — He says the operator usually owns this, whether the provider, the customer, or both. Customers need to know who is responsible and whether that party can do the job. ([4:48](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=288))
- **Cost per acceptable token is the producer's full spend over a period divided by the output customers can use, and better operations lowers it.** — Operations investment (testing, safe deployment, upkeep) has to be paid for by the extra output. An advantage persists only if the team repeats the process as workloads change. ([8:13](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=493))
- **Cost position is relative. Improving does not guarantee advantage if competitors improve faster.** — Hypothetical: your cost per successful task falls 10 to 8 while a competitor's falls 6 to 12. You started cheaper and ended more expensive, which gives the competitor room to cut prices. ([9:40](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=580))
- **Scarcity hides cost differences, so operators should build cost advantage during the boom while they can still raise capital.** — He draws on the history of commodity cycles. Demand exceeding supply can make producers with very different costs look equally successful until the cycle turns. ([11:07](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=667))
- **Compute futures need grades and a benchmark that tracks the rent actually paid. Rating the operator is one building block, not a complete solution.** — A rental price index measures an advertised or traded GPU-hour price, not production cost or intelligence delivered. A hedge works only if index changes track changes in the user's rent. Rating does not define a grade, create liquidity, or make a hedge effective by itself. ([12:31](https://youtu.be/3axY1wz2OvA?t=751))

## Techniques
- **Cost per acceptable token** — Count all spend to deliver the service over a period (rent, or equipment amortization plus running costs, plus software and staff), then divide by output that meets the customer's quality and latency requirements. Compare across producers on the same workload and quality bar. *When:* Comparing serving setups or providers, or deciding whether an ops investment pays off.
- **Compare on the same chip at a fixed interactivity target** — Hold GPU type, model, input/output token ratio (~8k in / ~1k out), and per-user output speed target (P50 100 tok/s) constant. Measure output tok/s at P90 across serving stacks and settings, then compare output per paid hour. *When:* Benchmarking serving stacks or evaluating a GPU rental offer. Use the SemiAnalysis public inference calculator for reference estimates.
- **Assess the operator, not just the hardware** — Score provisioning, reliability, monitoring and security. Include hands-on tests, documentation review and customer feedback, and check behavior during machine failures as well as on good days. Rate managed clusters separately from bare metal, where more ops work sits with the customer. *When:* Choosing a GPU provider, or deciding which operating work you must cover yourself.
- **Safe update and rollback process for serving** — When a faster serving update appears: test it on the customer's real workload, check the responses still meet requirements, load-test, deploy with a plan, keep a way back to the previous version, then maintain it and respond to incidents. *When:* Upgrading inference serving software in production.

## Hot takes
- Price per token is a practical proxy for delivered output, but the North Star is price per successfully completed task at the required quality.
- GPU-hour price is a measure of input (cost), not output, so GPU dollar per hour should not be treated as the price of intelligence.
- Being the cheapest producer today does not hold up if competitors improve faster, so operating advantage has to be re-earned continuously rather than established once.
- Rating the operator is necessary for a credible compute futures market, but it does not by itself define a futures grade, a benchmark, or an effective hedge.
- Operating capability is missing from chip performance metrics, and security (such as tenant isolation of monitoring data) is part of usefulness.
