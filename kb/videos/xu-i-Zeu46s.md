> Kai Explains essay on why US public opinion turned against AI and data centers: broken promises, local costs, and water as the proxy grievance. Opinion piece, not a technical talk.

## Summary
The host argues that the backlash against AI infrastructure is not a communication failure but a response to promises that did not materialize. He cites a claim that up to 75% of Americans oppose data center construction in their communities, with opposition spanning party, age and income. He says data center costs (noise, power draw, land, water) fall on host towns while the value accrues elsewhere, and that water became the vehicle for broader grievances such as distrust of distant corporations, NDAs with local governments and tax subsidies. He reports that some politicians reversed quickly, including a Texas governor directive said to have halted up to 1,800 projects. He says the industry made its costliest mistake by treating lived anecdotes about jobs, learning and loneliness as non-evidence, and that overselling (cures for cancer, democratized creativity) eroded trust. He ends without a clean answer and suggests leaders build one data center in their own neighborhood. Most figures are unsourced in the transcript.

## Key ideas
- **The opposition is broad and bipartisan** — The host cites up to 75% of Americans opposing data center construction in their communities, with opposition holding across gender, age, income and party. ([0:00](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=0))
- **Data center costs are geographically asymmetric** — He cites more than 700 large US data centers, 49 in Loudoun County VA and none in San Francisco. Noise, power draw, land and water costs land on host communities while the revenue is made elsewhere. ([1:15](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=75))
- **Water is a proxy for broader grievances** — He says data center water use is well below golf courses, alfalfa and lawns, but the water argument wins because it is tangible and needs no technical background. The real grievances are distrust of distant corporations, local economic dependence, NDAs with local governments and tax subsidies. ([2:27](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=147))
- **Politicians reversed fast, which signals electoral pressure, not principle** — He cites a Texas governor directive that reportedly halted up to 1,800 data center projects, Michigan Senate nominees backing one-year moratoriums, and Pennsylvania gubernatorial ads attacking opponents as pro data center. He reads this as electoral arithmetic. ([4:57](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=297))
- **Dismissing lived experience was the industry's costliest mistake** — When people raised jobs, student writing, online sameness or loneliness, the optimist answer was that they did not understand the research. He argues the people were describing their lives accurately and that anecdotes were treated as non-evidence. ([6:19](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=379))
- **Overselling eroded trust** — He recounts the pitch (cure cancer, democratize creativity, compress decades of progress), the unevenly distributed productivity gains, layoffs attributed to AI, and risks that were deferred but appeared immediately. He admits he repeated this pitch himself. ([8:55](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=535))
- **Proposed test: leaders should bear local costs** — He argues that if leaders believe the technology is transformative, a data center near their own home should be an acceptable price. He notes that none of the named leaders has done this, and says that choice reveals more than keynotes do. He does not offer a clean resolution. ([10:16](https://youtu.be/xu-i-Zeu46s?t=616))

## Techniques
- **Tangible-unit framing for infrastructure costs** — The speaker argues that a physical, specific unit (water, a bottle per email) spreads faster than an abstract one (kWh). Lead with a concrete unit people can picture, and verify it before repeating it. *When:* Explaining infra costs or siting trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders or community audiences.
- **Treat stated concerns as evidence before explaining** — Ask what the person experienced and record it as data, before offering research or demos. The speaker's point is that explaining to someone who has not asked to be taught signals they are not worth listening to. *When:* Responding to skepticism from users, customers, staff or community members about an AI rollout.
- **Cost-locality check on advocacy** — Ask whether the people promoting a build bear its local costs (noise, power, water, land). The speaker uses 'would you build one next to your own house' as a credibility test. *When:* Assessing whether an infrastructure or AI-adoption pitch is credible, or planning public messaging for a build.
