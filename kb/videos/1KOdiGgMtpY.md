> Lena Hall argues that when AI makes building cheap, value moves to choosing what to build and keeping that choice intact through shipping and marketing, using a thin "signal layer."

## Summary
Lena Hall (Akamai) argues that AI tools converge everyone on the same answers, so implementation is no longer a differentiator. What remains is deciding what to point at, which she says needs a real personal need or a close relationship with a domain. She says taste is learnable preference under feedback, so only narrow judgment about unseen events and private customer relationships resists imitation. Her second half is about shipping: the signal distorts at the source, through organizational handoffs, and through AI remixing launch copy. Her proposed fix is a small "thin signal layer" that welds each promise to its limit, keeps the limit visible in the product, and checks what readers actually understood. She closes by saying the goal is earned trust, which is not automatable, and that generic output costs real money and loses customers.

## Key ideas
- **Implementation is converging for everyone, so the scarce skill is choosing what to build.** — She says the best coding agents went from solving a fraction of a standard software benchmark to the high 80s in about two years, but shipping barely moved. Her reading is that benchmarks only measure the parts of software work that have an automatic grader, and shipping brings the ungraded parts back. Anything a model can grade itself on gets trained against. ([3:33](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=213))
- **Your own need is the best signal for what to build, though it is necessary and not sufficient.** — She cites Paul Graham's advice to build what you and your friends need because the market has not formed yet. She uses Twitch as an example of an idea that sounded silly but worked, and notes that many similar weird ideas failed. ([5:00](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=300))
- **Broad taste is not a moat, because taste is preference under feedback that a model can imitate.** — She says only two kinds of judgment resist training: judgment about events with no data yet, and judgment built on a relationship with a specific customer and their history that a model has read about but never met. Richard Hamming's idea of keeping a list of important problems in mind, and acting when a new tool gives you an attack, comes in here. ([6:24](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=384))
- **Use AI in two different ways: as an average-prompt generator, or as a converger around your own point of view.** — An average prompt produces output that looks like everyone else's. Her better approach is to bring the specific point of view and the real story, then let AI handle drafting, formatting, and cleanup around that core. ([10:15](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=615))
- **The signal distorts in three places: at the source, across organizational handoffs, and through AI remixing.** — Source distortion happens when founders compress context their audience does not have, so the pitch starts with architecture and skips the customer pain. Organizational distortion happens at every handoff, where the signal drifts toward the average. Machine distortion happens when AI turns a careful, narrow launch claim into a broad promise. ([11:38](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=698))
- **Fix distortion with a thin signal layer, not more process layers.** — She describes a small, deliberate function whose only job is to validate that the original intent survives each handoff, reattaching people to the outcome the way a founder is. She says adding more process layers makes the problem worse. ([13:00](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=780))
- **Weld the promise to its limit, and keep the limit visible in the product and in the launch.** — Her monitoring-tool example promises to stay quiet on alerts it cannot tie to real user impact, and to show every silenced alert so a user can override it. She says a launch number like '90% fewer pages' must sit next to the statement that every silence is visible and reversible, so AI remixing cannot drop the limit. ([15:33](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=933))
- **Test what people heard before scaling a launch, by having a stranger describe the product back to you.** — Give a README to someone who has never seen the project, then ask them to explain it in their own words. The gap between their description and what you meant is the distortion you were about to broadcast. She says much of this checking can be automated. ([16:58](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=1018))
- **Trust is the outcome AI cannot replace, and generic output has a real cost.** — Trust is granted slowly through a relationship with consent, and there is no benchmark or reward signal for it. She says averageness costs tokens, infra, and salaried hours, and that every generic post teaches a reader not to click again. ([18:12](https://youtu.be/1KOdiGgMtpY?t=1092))

## Techniques
- **Stranger readme read-back check** — Hand a README or launch page to someone unfamiliar with the project and ask them to describe the product back. Compare their description with the intended one and fix the gaps before you broadcast. *When:* Before scaling launch content or a product pitch, especially after AI has drafted or reshaped it.
- **Promise welded to its limit** — Write the product claim as one sentence with the scope built in, for example 'stays quiet on anything it cannot tie to real user impact and shows everything it silenced'. Put the limit in the product UI, such as a visible list of suppressed items, so it cannot be cut from the copy. *When:* When a product's main claim could be read as broader than what it actually does, or when AI will shorten the copy.
- **Human point of view, AI for the rest** — Write the core point of view and the real story yourself, then have the model handle drafting, formatting, and cleanup around that core. Do not ask it to generate the point of view from a short prompt. *When:* When creating posts, launch copy, or decks where differentiation matters.
- **Thin signal layer across handoffs** — Add one small step, owned by someone close to the outcome, whose only job is to check that the original intent still holds at each handoff between teams. Do not add general process layers to do this. *When:* When a signal passes through several departments such as product, legal, sales, and marketing, and the output keeps drifting toward generic.
- **Hamming list of important problems** — Keep 10 to 20 important problems in mind. When a new tool or angle gives you a real attack on one, act on it. *When:* When choosing what to work on next, and a new capability has just appeared.

## Hot takes
- When anything can be built, speed is no longer where value comes from; choosing what to point at is.
- Broad good taste is not a differentiator, because preference under feedback is exactly what models learn to imitate.
- Generic AI-written content has a real cost: it teaches readers not to come back, so it makes you harder to choose.
- Adding more process layers to fix signal loss usually makes the problem worse.
