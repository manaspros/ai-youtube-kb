> Fireside chat between a hedge fund founder and a Kepler co-founder on how AI changes stock picking: AI raises the baseline, auditability is the trust gate, and edge comes from divergent views and idea velocity.

## Summary
Jack Woodruff (founder and former CIO of Candlestick Capital, a long/short equity fund, ex-Surveyor Capital/Citadel) talks with Vinoo Ganesh (Kepler) about fundamental investing in the AI era. His core claim is that alpha still comes from divergent views built from a mosaic of non-obvious data points, while the tools change how information is synthesized and what counts as table stakes. He argues AI can automate preparation work (he cites cutting 6-8 hours of prep to about 5 minutes) but that freed time gets reinvested into proprietary research, so the bar rises. He stresses that humans remain the bottleneck and that every number must be auditable back to its source before anyone trusts it. On fund design he expects analyst pyramids to flatten toward fewer preparers and more producers, and he argues idea velocity and hit rate matter most when a firm can cover more names. He is skeptical that fully autonomous no-analyst AI funds will beat human funds, since whoever has the most compute and information wins once humans leave the loop. The talk gives practitioner opinion rather than technical detail, and one speaker is from a vendor company.

## Key ideas
- **Long-term stock picking fundamentals stay the same; alpha still comes from a divergent view, but how information is synthesized and what counts as table stakes will change completely.** — The speaker expects PM and analyst roles to be turned upside down, but says it is too early to know what they will look like. ([0:08](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=8))
- **Investment management is a time-management problem, and humans are the bottleneck.** — Returns on time spent per stock diminish, so AI matters if it can cover more names or cut prep time. The speaker cites prep that took 6-8 hours now being done in about 5 minutes. ([2:50](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=170))
- **Automating prep does not free people to do less work; it raises the bar for everyone.** — Competitors who work the same way get commoditized, so the freed time goes to proprietary research, channel checks and industry-backdrop work to find a divergent view. ([4:10](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=250))
- **The most valuable use of AI is directing human attention to the right names and projects, not producing buy or sell decisions.** — Tools should flag where to spend time, such as special projects that need deep education, rather than output a trade. Those flagged projects are where the edge is found before others do. ([5:35](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=335))
- **Alpha comes from a mosaic of three or four non-obvious data points that line up across different sources.** — The speaker treats this as the widely accepted view of how alpha is created, so a single data point is not the edge. ([6:50](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=410))
- **Auditability of every number is the gate to trusting AI output, and it is the main reason he would not let AI run trading decisions.** — He would not sign off on a model output without seeing where each number came from, and notes that people still spend time auditing AI-built models, which undercuts the time saved. ([6:50](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=410))
- **Data isolation and security are the main concerns when a fund evaluates AI vendors.** — The speaker defers this to his CTO, but says internal data must not leave the firm. He describes the next phase as automation and data build-up first, then the interesting uses. ([9:21](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=561))
- **Once every fund has the same models and information, edge comes from better prompting, faster man-machine combination and better questions.** — Competitive moats in investing are short-lived because other smart, motivated people find the same arbitrage. ([10:32](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=632))
- **Fund structure may become more vertical: fewer preparers, more producers, and more non-analyst roles such as investigative and project-research staff and data engineers.** — The old pyramid had many analysts feeding senior pickers, PMs and a CIO. The speaker still wants the people who make the money and might keep preparers for proprietary feed data. ([14:25](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=865))
- **Idea velocity and hit rate are the core metric for a fund, and the number of covered names changes the concentration of the book.** — A small team has slower idea velocity and a concentrated book. A large team with more names can pick a smaller share of ideas, but research quality has to hold across all names. ([15:52](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=952))
- **A fully autonomous no-analyst AI fund will only replicate last year's fund, and taking humans out of the loop reduces the edge to whoever has the most compute and information.** — The speaker does not think humans can be removed from fundamental investing, unlike quantitative funds that removed them decades ago. ([17:14](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=1034))

## Techniques
- **Audit-first verification of model-produced numbers** — Do not trust a model-produced figure until its source is traceable and checked. Keep the data auditable end to end. The speaker says he would not sign off on a number he could not trace, and that people still re-audit AI-built models, which cuts into the time saved. *When:* Any pipeline where a wrong number can cost real money, such as financial models, data entry, or trading inputs. Use it before you automate the output.
- **AI as attention triage for human research** — Use the agent to rank or flag which names, projects or questions deserve deep human work, instead of asking it for the final decision. The speaker frames the output as a list of projects to investigate, not trade ideas. *When:* When human research capacity is the bottleneck and the decision still needs human judgment.
- **Automate prep, reinvest time in differentiated work** — Let AI do the standard preparation work, such as summarizing filings and transcripts or cleaning data, and move the freed hours into work nobody else is doing. The speaker warns that if the team keeps the same workflow, it becomes table stakes. *When:* When a standard analysis is widely done by competitors and speed alone no longer differentiates.

## Numbers
- Historical comparison: 18 people covered about 400 stocks at Citadel, and the speaker's Candlestick team had 30 stock pickers. ([1:33](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=93))
- Prep work the speaker says took 6-8 hours can be done by an AI program in about 5 minutes (his example, not a measurement). ([2:50](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=170))
- Portfolio math: with 20 holdings, the chance of picking any given name is 20% of 100 covered names, 10% of 200, and 5% of 400. ([15:52](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=952))
- Example turnover of a high-frequency book: about 9,000 turns per year. ([14:25](https://youtu.be/y2dyD6slKxE?t=865))

## Hot takes
- No autonomous no-analyst AI fund will beat human-led funds, because whoever has the most compute and information wins once humans are out of the loop.
- Auditability of every number matters more than model accuracy gains; trust is the gate for adoption in finance.
- Vendor-adjacent framing: one speaker is from Kepler and makes Kepler's auditability pitch, and the buyer and CTO questions are sales-oriented. Treat the product claims as unverified.
- Long-term fundamental stock picking stays the same; the table-stakes baseline is what moves.
