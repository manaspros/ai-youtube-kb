> Engineering leader David Pavlik on hiring, motivating and retaining top engineers, drawing on Netflix, SpaceX, Microsoft and Amazon: mission and people beat perks, and hiring is the manager's job.

## Summary
David Pavlik, a long-time engineering leader, talks about what he calls 10x or 100x engineers and how to hire, motivate and keep them, based on his time at Microsoft, Netflix, SpaceX, Amazon and ShipMonk. His central claim is that top people are driven by a clear mission and the product or project they can deliver, not by salary, benefits or work-life balance. On hiring, he argues the hiring manager (or founder) owns it, that candidates should be shown something extra about the company, that interviews should be collaborative rather than adversarial, and that outside "bar raisers" help counter pressure-driven bias. For retention, he recommends checking whether mission pivots still align the team, watching for life events and adjusting benefits, not forcing strong engineers into management, and using stock refreshers rather than relying on salary raises. The talk is anecdotal and light on method, but the specific practices (paid trials, bar raisers, the step-back management path) are concrete.

## Key ideas
- **Define what 10x or 100x engineers means for your own company and what characteristics they have, before trying to hire or keep them.** — He frames the topic around the people who are most impactful, using examples like Netflix's 'stunning colleagues', SpaceX's 'kick-ass award' and ShipMonk's 'owners'. He asks each company to name its own version. ([0:00](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=0))
- **Hiring is the hiring manager's responsibility (the founder's at a startup), not the recruiting team's; recruiters help, but the manager owns the outcome.** — Netflix's view was that a manager has two jobs: get the best people to work with, and remove the obstacles the team faces. ([7:40](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=460))
- **Recruit with something extra and a clear, bold mission, since the vision and a visible path to contribute are what convince strong candidates.** — He was recruited by SpaceX with a pitch about putting people on Mars and by Netflix to run chaos engineering. SpaceX also invited his wife for a tour and dinner, which he credits with getting him the job on the second try. ([3:42](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=222))
- **Use paid, collaborative trial work instead of adversarial coding interviews; let the candidate see how the team works and organizes its code.** — He cites a Stripe CTO podcast on collaborative interviews, and says the interview is also the candidate's chance to evaluate the company. ([10:16](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=616))
- **Bring in outside 'bar raisers' to counter hiring bias when a team is under pressure to hire fast.** — Amazon's bar raisers are senior people from outside the team who must agree on a hire with the hiring manager. He describes meetings where the bar raiser said no and had to argue the case with the manager. ([11:17](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=677))
- **Motivation for top people is the product, the mission and the people they work with. Salary and perks are a lower priority and do not replace purpose.** — He describes a hierarchy in which product, company mission and people sit above benefits. Teams have also followed a leader from one company to the next (a Netflix team that moved on to Apple and Google). ([14:08](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=848))
- **Ask candidates what motivates them, and align the pitch with the answer; engagement means engagement with the mission, not a long survey.** — He suggests the question 'what motivates you to work here?' during interviews, and treats 'do you have a best friend at work?' as a useful engagement signal. ([15:33](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=933))
- **When the mission pivots, re-check that the team still wants the new work, because people may leave and both sides become frustrated.** — This is his retention point about mission alignment. He notes that a pivot is fine as long as the team is considered. ([16:53](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=1013))
- **Retention means tracking life-changing events (families, kids) and adjusting the environment, such as childcare and schools, as the team grows.** — He describes SpaceX adding kindergarten, schools and daycare over the years as its early staff had children. He advises startups to ask whether anything has changed in team members' lives after about five years. ([18:18](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=1098))
- **Don't default to promoting your strongest engineers into management; keep a route back to the individual-contributor track.** — He recounts a strong engineer promoted to manage about 20 people who asked to step back after two months. He recommends an explicit option to return to the IC path. ([19:41](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=1181))
- **Keep people on projects they care about, and use stock refreshers, not just salary raises, to keep people after the first vesting period.** — He says people get bored or move on when a project finishes with nothing they are passionate about next. He criticises four-year grant schedules with no refresh, and says stock is often the most important compensation component in fast-growing companies. ([21:07](https://youtu.be/yr_8HIZNQOw?t=1267))

## Techniques
- **Paid collaborative trial instead of a coding interview** — Invite the candidate to work on a real project with the team, on site or remotely, so the session tests how they collaborate and gives them a view of the codebase, repo organisation and processes. Aim to solve a problem together rather than trying to catch the candidate out. *When:* Hiring experienced engineers where fit and collaboration matter more than puzzle performance, and where the company wants candidates to judge the team too.
- **Outside bar raiser in the hiring loop** — Assign a senior interviewer from outside the hiring team, with hiring experience, who must agree on a hire along with the hiring manager. Use them to counter urgency bias when the team is desperate to fill roles. *When:* A company that is big enough to have a separate hiring function, and whenever the team is under pressure to hire quickly.
- **Ask what motivates the candidate, then align the pitch** — In the interview, ask 'what is motivating you to work for our company?' Accept any answer (salary, benefits, product, people) and check whether it matches what the role actually offers. Mismatched motivation predicts the hire will not last. *When:* Late-stage interviews, or when you need to decide between candidates with similar skill.
- **Recruit with an extra-mile experience** — Make the candidate's experience of the process memorable: invite a partner, show the workplace, arrange peer dinners. The point is to go beyond the minimum so strong candidates choose you. *When:* Competing for scarce senior talent.
- **Engagement check via a best-friend-at-work question** — Include 'do you have a best friend at work?' in engagement surveys and use it as a rough signal of belonging, alongside mission-focused questions rather than a long questionnaire. *When:* Running periodic engagement checks as the team grows.
- **Re-validate team alignment after a mission pivot** — After changing the company's direction, check with each team member whether they still want to work on the new thing. Do not assume the existing team will follow the pivot. *When:* A pivot or significant change to product or mission.
- **Life-event review and environment adjustment** — Periodically ask what has changed in team members' lives (families, children, relocation) and adjust working arrangements, benefits or support such as childcare to match. *When:* A company past its first few years, with a team that has grown up and started families.
- **Dual-track promotion with a step-back path** — Before promoting a strong engineer into management, make clear that they can return to the individual-contributor track if it does not suit them. Keep that door open rather than treating the move as one-way. *When:* Offering a management promotion to a high-performing IC.
- **Stock refresher grants instead of four-year cliffs** — Issue refresher equity during the vesting schedule so that people keep a stake in the company's success after the initial grant, and do not rely only on salary increases. *When:* Fast-growing startups or scale-ups where equity is a major part of pay.

## Hot takes
- Motivation does not equal salary, benefits or work-life balance, and top talent will skip perks for purpose and mission. This rests on the speaker's experience at high-profile companies and may not hold for every candidate pool.
- The hiring manager, not the recruiting team, owns hiring, and a bar raiser from outside the team should be able to block a hire.
- Your best individual contributors should not be promoted into management by default, and the company should offer an explicit route back to the IC track.
- Stock refreshers matter more than salary raises for retaining people at startups, and four-year grants with no refresh are a poor retention design.
