> Charles Frye (Modal) explains what runs inside an LLM inference engine: workloads, prefill vs decode, process layout, scheduler bottlenecks, KV cache, CUDA graphs, speculative decoding, and production debugging.

## Summary
Frye frames inference as the revenue-generating part of the AI stack and splits serving workloads into chatbot-plus, background agents and data processors, each with different latency and throughput needs. He breaks an engine into IO, tokenizer/detokenizer, a single-threaded scheduler and GPU model runners, and says the scheduler, not the GPU kernels, is where most engines differ and fail. The core techniques he covers are prefix/radix KV caching, CUDA graph capture to remove host-side launch and sync overhead, and speculative decoding, which keeps the target model's output while speeding decode. He ends with production advice: treat queueing and cache thrash as failure states, log token IDs and many metrics, run evals against the live deployment, and read a dashboard by checking TTFT first and inter-token latency second. A closing segment promotes Modal's deployment product and points to MiniSGLang, NanoVLM and DeepWiki as learning resources. Speaker asked the audience to interrupt with questions, so much of the talk is Q&A-driven.

## Key ideas
- **Inference is a revenue center while training is a cost center, so the money is in running models well.** — Speaker says labs rarely sell raw weights as a business, but people pay to send tokens in and get tokens out. Inference engineers deploy and customize foundation models. Post-training also relies on inference-style sampling, so the same engine concerns show up there. ([4:36](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=276))
- **Serving workloads fall into three archetypes with different latency and throughput targets.** — Chatbot-plus (a person waiting, plus tool calls), background agents (minutes to hours, no tight latency budget), and data processors (small models turning documents into structured rows, throughput-bound with little prefix reuse). ([9:01](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=541))
- **Each request is two sub-workloads: prefill is compute-dense, decode is memory-bound.** — Prefill runs one forward pass over the whole prompt with high arithmetic intensity. Decode produces one or a few tokens per step and does far less math per user per step. They follow different code paths and need different tuning, and the split defines the metrics that matter: time to first token and inter-token latency. ([14:19](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=859))
- **An engine is stateless only until you care about performance; then the KV cache is the state that matters.** — Accumulated KV cache tied to a session is the main state. Defining sessions and routing them to replicas is the job of a wrapping layer such as Dynamo or llm-d, not the engine. ([19:19](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=1159))
- **The scheduler is a semantically single-threaded gatekeeper to the GPU and becomes the bottleneck as GPUs get faster.** — Its CPU work is small, but it decides what runs on the GPU and manages its resources. Speaker argues that parallelizing schedulers adds locking complexity that does not pay off, because GPU steps take tens to hundreds of ms, so host-side gains of microseconds are not useful unless the GPU is also waiting on the host. ([21:30](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=1290))
- **Engines differ mainly in host-side batching and scheduling, not in raw GPU kernels, which are mostly borrowed from shared libraries.** — Engines pull kernels from libraries like FlashAttention, FlashInfer, CUTLASS, Triton and DeepGEMM. Differentiation comes from batch construction, queue design and getting out of the GPU's way. A Rust rewrite of the host scheduler is a plausible future target because it is per-engine and host-only. ([36:56](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=2216))
- **Queueing and KV-cache thrashing are failure states of a deployment, not normal operation.** — Swapping cache to CPU memory or disk is a safety valve, comparable to OS swap that makes a laptop unusable. The fix is to add replicas so queues drain. Under a traffic spike, time to first token rises first from prefill queueing, then inter-token latency rises from GPU contention. ([45:29](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=2729))
- **Speculative decoding preserves the target model's output and gives a speedup that scales with speculator quality.** — A small speculator drafts several tokens and the target model verifies them in parallel with rejection sampling, so the result matches sequential sampling up to numerics. Better speculators that accept more tokens per step give near-linear gains, which speaker says makes model training a lever for decode speed. ([50:26](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=3026))
- **Model-quality bugs are the engine's responsibility, and tokenizer and chat-template bugs are common right after a model release.** — Speaker recommends running the engine's benchmark or eval scripts against the deployed endpoint, logging token IDs next to the text, and logging as many metrics as you can, because you do not know which cross-correlation will reveal a bug. ([54:05](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=3245))

## Techniques
- **Phase-split serving metrics** — Measure time to first token (prefill-bound) and inter-token latency (decode-bound) separately per request. Also log prefill and decode token counts, prefix-reuse rate, and queries per second per replica. Map each workload to its SLO. *When:* Sizing a deployment, choosing replica counts, or diagnosing a latency regression.
- **Keep host work off the GPU critical path** — Look for host-device syncs in the scheduler and model loop, which block progress. Capture the decode-step kernels as a CUDA graph so many per-kernel CPU launches become one graph launch. *When:* GPU utilization has gaps, or tokens per second stalls even though the GPU is fast.
- **Speculative decoding with exact-output verification** — A speculator model drafts several tokens, the target model scores them all in one parallel pass, and rejection sampling keeps the output equal to sequential sampling. Invest in a better speculator to accept more tokens per step. *When:* Decode-bound latency targets on a fixed set of GPUs, and you have budget to train a speculator.
- **Prefix and radix KV-cache reuse** — Store computed KV blocks in a paged or radix-tree layout so requests sharing a prefix reuse the computation. Speaker notes that attention kernels now read this layout directly, so capacity management and cache layout are the engine's job. *When:* Agent workloads with large shared system prompts (tens of thousands of tokens) across many users.
- **Treat queueing and cache thrash as alerts** — Scale out replicas when time to first token climbs. Avoid depending on swapping KV cache to CPU or disk. Keep queues draining rather than growing. *When:* Autoscaled production inference under bursty traffic.
- **Correctness logging and post-deploy evals** — Log token IDs alongside text for tokenizer debugging. Run the engine's benchmark or eval scripts against the live endpoint on every deploy or optimization, and log traces so issues can be traced back to specific requests. *When:* After a model release, after a performance optimization, or when replicas behave differently.
- **Profile for per-GPU or per-host anomalies** — Use Nsight Systems or the PyTorch profiler to see all processes and GPU work in one timeline. Compare replicas to spot a single GPU running slow. *When:* One replica or GPU is consistently slower than others. Speaker's example was a NUMA placement problem.

## Numbers
- Speaker has helped teams deploy and optimize inference on hundreds or thousands of GPUs on Modal. ([5:51](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=351))
- Chatbot-plus outputs are tens to hundreds of tokens with a first-token budget of a few hundred milliseconds at most. Background agents get minutes to a few hours. ([12:26](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=746))
- A 100k-token prompt is roughly 10x the prefill work of a 10k-token prompt, and cost is roughly linear in input length past a certain size (speaker's rough estimate). ([29:33](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=1773))
- Tokenizer latency is millisecond-scale and happens once per request. It rarely bottlenecks models around 20B parameters or larger, but can for small models with very long inputs. ([32:11](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=1931))
- At about 1,000 output tokens per second, each decode step takes about 1 ms on average. Large MoE models at lower decode throughput can take 10 to 20 ms or more per token. ([32:11](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=1931))
- GPU work per scheduler step is generally tens to hundreds of milliseconds, so host-side scheduler savings in microseconds would not reach the GPU. ([24:26](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=1466))
- Decode may load roughly 100 GB to 1 TB of weights per token step (speaker's rough figure), producing petabyte-per-second-scale bandwidth demand at a token every couple of milliseconds. ([50:26](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=3026))
- With speculative decoding and a good speculator, fairly large models can reach about 1,000 tokens per second on NVIDIA hardware. Speaker cites 2x, 4x or 8x speedups over baseline as achievable with better speculators. ([53:00](https://youtu.be/woIYJYd_etI?t=3180))

## Hot takes
- Inference is the revenue center and training is the cost center, because model weights are hard to sell directly.
- Raw kernel performance is similar across engines. The real differences are how well the engine stays out of the GPU's way.
- Queueing in production should be treated as a failure state. A growing queue means the deployment is under-provisioned.
- Tokenizer caching is an overlooked optimization that nobody talks about even though it could be done.
- The scheduler is a good target for a Rust rewrite, since it is host-only and per-engine.
