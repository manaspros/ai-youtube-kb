> A llama.cpp PR caches individual MoE experts in spare VRAM for a claimed 4x decode speedup; the real gain over a tuned baseline is about 1.5-2.2x and depends on hit rate, PCIe link and workload.

## Summary
The video walks through llama.cpp PR 29887 (merged October 7, 2026 by a developer named Lich77), which keeps a least-recently-used cache of individual MoE expert weights in spare VRAM instead of offloading whole expert layers to system RAM. On the same RTX card and 4-bit 125B MoE model, decode went from 9.7 to 37.2 tokens per second, but that 3.8x headline is measured against a baseline with no experts in VRAM. Against an optimized whole-layer split, the speaker estimates 1.5x to 2.2x, and the gap between the experimental branch and mainline is partly pinned-memory and CPU-routing overhead. The cache only pays off when the hit rate is high (break-even around 85%, worthwhile above 90%), which requires roughly a quarter of the expert weights in VRAM. Slow PCIe links, small VRAM, prompt-heavy workloads and speculative decoding can all make it slower. The speaker gives a 15-minute benchmarking procedure and notes that the specialist runtime Strata beat llama.cpp on this one model family in a single head-to-head run.

## Key ideas
- **llama.cpp PR 29887 (merged Oct 7) caches individual experts in spare VRAM. On the same card and model, decode went from 9.7 to 37.2 tok/s, which the video rounds to a 3.8x (headline 4x) gain.** — The contribution is by a developer named Lich77 (also rendered as Leech77 in the captions). The comparison is between the same card and same 4-bit quantization, so the gain reflects the feature rather than a different model. ([1:23](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=83))
- **MoE sparsity is the reason per-expert caching helps. The model has 48 layers, 512 experts per layer, and routes each token to 10 experts, so roughly 2% of expert weights are active per token.** — Most of the expert weights sit idle at any moment, so spending VRAM on whole layers stores mostly unused experts. ([2:46](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=166))
- **Offloading experts to system RAM makes data movement the bottleneck. Each token needs about 48 CPU-to-GPU handoffs, and the speaker estimates roughly half of generation time is spent moving data over PCIe.** — The existing --n-cpu-moe style of whole-layer placement fits only four or five layers on a 24 GB card, and the rest stay on the CPU. ([5:01](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=301))
- **The expert cache is an LRU pool of individual experts (about 2.9 MB each at 4-bit) with a hit rate as the key metric. Routing locality means frequently used experts stay resident, and only misses are copied over the bus.** — The speaker argues hit rate matters more than teraflops, RAM speed or quantization level for real-world decode speed. ([7:32](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=452))
- **The 4x headline is measured from a zero-expert-in-VRAM baseline. Against optimized whole-layer placement, the speaker cites 2.2x from llama.cpp's own docs on an RTX 5090 and 1.5x for the experimental branch versus mainline, with pinned memory and per-token CPU routing round trips as the main gaps.** — Pinned host memory copied at about 24 GB/s vs 9 GB/s unpinned, moving one 49.4 to 58.1 tok/s. In the mainline routing path, 13-14 ms of each 17-19 ms token was CPU handoff time. ([10:10](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=610))
- **A later NVIDIA patch (PR 30449, Oct 16) fixed Windows silently dropping large pinned-memory requests. On an RTX 5090 with multi-token prediction it moved decode from 37.76 to 62.73 tok/s.** — The speaker says the routing-loop issue was still open and no full head-to-head rerun had been published since then, so the comparison is still moving. ([12:38](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=758))
- **Sizing is the decisive factor. About 65 GB of 4-bit expert weights needs roughly 16 GB of cache (a quarter) to reach a useful hit rate, on top of attention and context memory. Small cards tested in the video lost speed with every cache size.** — An 8 GB laptop 4060 dropped from 23.4 to 15 tok/s with a 1.5 GB cache. A 12 GB 4070 Super went 14.8 to 12.9 (4 GB cache) and 11 (2 GB cache). A 12 GB 3060 with an 8 GB cache was also slower. Two 5090 owners with large VRAM reached 27 to 50.8 tok/s and 2324 to 4045 at long contexts. ([13:51](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=831))
- **The break-even hit rate is about 85%. Below that, the cost of copying misses exceeds computing those experts on the CPU, and a 0.5 GB cache on an 80B Qwen model was 24% slower than no cache.** — The speaker's bands: under 85% the cache costs speed, 85-90% is a gray zone, above 90% it pays, and 95% or more is strong. ([15:04](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=904))
- **The PCIe link is a hidden limit. A PCIe 4.0 x16 slot is about 31.5 GB/s, while a chipset-wired x4 slot is about 7.9 GB/s, which caps decode near 58 tok/s even at a 90% hit rate. On a PCIe 3.0 2080 Ti, whole-layer placement beat the cache.** — At a 90% hit rate on this model, each token moves about 137 MB across the bus. The speaker suggests checking the link width and speed in nvidia-smi before benchmarking. ([17:19](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=1039))
- **The cache helps decode but can hurt prompt processing and speculative decoding. On a 2x RTX 4090 test, decode was 1.85x faster but prompt reading fell to 0.73x. Speculative tokens each pick their own experts, which can flood a cache sized for one token at a time.** — One developer measured multi-token prediction 27% slower with experts in RAM, while the experimental branch got about 20% on top of the cache with a large enough pool. The speaker also reports a repetition loop that appeared only with the cache on, which is possibly unrelated. ([18:35](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=1115))
- **For this model family, the specialist runtime Strata beat llama.cpp in the one head-to-head run shown: 85.8 tok/s with MTP vs 58.1, and prompt reading at 2320 vs 451 tok/s. The speaker still recommends llama.cpp as the generalist choice for switching models.** — A 12 GB RTX 5070 running Strata's 3-bit build reached 62 tok/s. The comparison was on one machine and a single run, so the speaker presents it as a lead to investigate rather than a settled result. ([24:19](https://youtu.be/shl8FFSoANE?t=1459))

## Techniques
- **Per-expert LRU cache in VRAM instead of whole-layer offload** — Reserve a VRAM pool (for example 16 GB). Keep the most recently used experts there, copy only missed experts from RAM over PCIe on demand, and evict the least recently used one. This is an llama.cpp PR 29887 feature, enabled with a cache-size flag. The name is garbled in the captions as 'CMOE' and the exact flag spelling should be checked in the llama.cpp docs. *When:* Use it on a large-VRAM card (24 GB or more, ideally 32 GB) running an MoE model whose expert weights are much larger than VRAM, with a fast PCIe x16 link and a generation-heavy workload. Skip it for small-VRAM cards or prompt-heavy workflows.
- **Go/no-go on hit rate from llama.cpp logs** — Run with the log level set to 4 and read the expert-cache hit rate after the run. Under 85%, the cache costs speed. 85-90% is a gray zone. Above 90% it pays off, and 95% or more is strong. *When:* Before deciding whether to keep the cache enabled, and when comparing cache sizes.
- **15-minute self-benchmark protocol** — (1) Update llama.cpp to release B11480 or newer (the speaker cites release 0.7 from October 15 and NVIDIA's October 16 pinned-memory patch). (2) Run with automatic GPU assignment or hand-tuned --n-cpu-moe until VRAM is nearly full, and record decode tokens per second and prompt tokens per second. (3) Add the cache flag and set the cache size to most of the free VRAM, leaving a few hundred MB for the OS and context. Run 2-3 warm-up prompts before measuring. (4) Set log level 4 and read the hit rate. (5) Compare against the best tuned whole-layer split from step 2, not against zero. *When:* Whenever deciding whether a cache, offload or placement setting helps a specific card, model and workflow. The speaker says trusting someone else's benchmark from different hardware is the failure mode.
- **Check the PCIe link before enabling expert offload** — Run nvidia-smi and read the current link width and current link speed for the GPU. An x4 or Gen3 link is a warning sign for cache-based offload, because the bus carries every miss. *When:* Before benchmarking any expert offload or cache setup on a multi-GPU or consumer motherboard where the secondary slot is chipset-wired.
- **Benchmark the actual workflow, not just decode speed** — Measure prompt processing (prefill) separately from decode. Test with the context size and speculative or multi-token prediction settings you actually use. The expert cache only runs during decode, on batches of 32 tokens or fewer. *When:* For coding agents or research workflows that resend large contexts on every turn, where slower prefill can cancel out faster generation.
- **Disable the cache first when a model starts looping** — If the output repeats a word or phrase, turn the expert cache off and rerun before debugging further. The speaker says a looping report appeared only with the cache enabled, but the cause was not proven. *When:* Whenever repetition appears after enabling the expert cache.
- **Sizing rule of thumb** — After attention weights and context are accounted for, dedicate about a quarter of the total expert weight size to the cache. If that isn't available, don't enable it. For a 4-bit model with about 65 GB of experts, that is about 16 GB. *When:* Deciding whether a given GPU is worth enabling per-expert caching on at all.

## Hot takes
- The 4x headline measures going from zero experts in VRAM to a third of the model's experts in VRAM. The fair comparison against tuned whole-layer placement is 1.5-2.2x.
- Enabling the expert cache on a small-VRAM card or with too small a pool is worse than no cache, because miss overhead outweighs CPU compute. Most viewers with 8-12 GB cards should not enable it.
- Benchmark numbers from different hardware or cherry-picked pull request threads are not reliable; measure hit rate and prompt speed on your own machine.
- Strata is faster than llama.cpp for this one model family, but it only runs that family, while llama.cpp supports new models quickly.
