> Google DeepMind researcher explains text diffusion as a low-latency, non-autoregressive alternative to next-token LLMs, covering why it is fast, self-correcting, and adaptive, plus its throughput cost.

## Summary
Brendan O'Donoghue (Google DeepMind) gives a research overview of text diffusion, where a model learns to denoise a corrupted token sequence and generates by starting from random tokens and refining them over a few passes. He ties the team's year-old Gemini Diffusion research demo to a comparison with Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite, reporting similar quality on most areas and better latency. The core argument is hardware-based: autoregressive decoding is memory-bandwidth bound, so generating a block in far fewer weight-streaming passes gives a large latency win. He claims three further properties that follow from bidirectional attention and iterative refinement: self-correction, quality that rises with more denoising steps, and adaptive compute where the model decides how long to spend. The main drawback he admits is lower throughput at large batch sizes, which makes it expensive to serve, so he expects on-device or latency-sensitive uses first. Most evidence is demos and a few internal eval remarks rather than published numbers, and he warns the figures are a year old.

## Key ideas
- **Text diffusion trains a network to remove discrete token corruption at several noise levels, then generates by starting from pure random tokens and iteratively refining the whole block.** — Unlike next-token decoding, the whole canvas (tens to thousands of tokens) is refined together over a few denoising steps, and each step can attend to future positions. ([0:00](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=0))
- **Text diffusion is faster per user because autoregressive decoding is memory-bandwidth bound.** — Each decoded token streams all weights and KV cache from HBM. If 256 tokens take about 24 forward passes, the model streams weights roughly 10x fewer times, which he argues gives about a 10x speedup when truly memory bound. ([7:50](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=470))
- **The latency advantage comes with a throughput cost that keeps diffusion out of large served models.** — Batched autoregressive serving makes good use of the accelerator. Diffusion does multiple passes over the same data, so it hits compute limits sooner and costs more per query at scale. He says no one is putting it into big models for this reason. ([3:55](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=235))
- **Bidirectional attention lets the model self-correct after it has reasoned to the end.** — In his square-root demo, Gemini Diffusion first guessed 60, then 49, and after a few passes that include the full reasoning it revised the start to 39. He says GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Flash both made errors on the same prompt, and Flash kept its wrong answer. ([9:15](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=555))
- **Quality generally improves as the denoising step budget increases, which makes steps a test-time compute knob.** — On six internal coding evals he describes the trend as roughly monotonic, not strictly so. Extra steps let the model re-read an almost-correct draft and fix errors. ([11:58](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=718))
- **The model learns to decide its own stopping point, so easy prompts use fewer steps than hard ones.** — Examples given: first 100 digits of pi in 4 steps, FizzBuzz code in 18, a single-paragraph quantum mechanics explanation in 31. He says hard evals like GPQA Diamond took longer than easy ones like MBPP. ([13:19](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=799))
- **In-place editing falls out of seeing the whole canvas: the model can fill a span in the middle of existing text or code.** — Demos include fixing a bug in place, adding documentation, and inserting a middle paragraph that stays consistent with the surrounding story. The method is not a left-to-right rewrite. ([14:33](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=873))
- **Low latency enables new interaction patterns, shown through demos rather than a measured product.** — Demos include on-the-fly generated Wikipedia and Reddit pages (with a separate image model for pictures) and an OS that generates each screen on click. An outside user built a voice-driven to-do app in what the speaker calls about 15 seconds. ([15:52](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=952))
- **Near-term use cases are on-device or latency-sensitive settings where batch throughput does not matter.** — He says the team already has on-device uses inside the Alphabet ecosystem, such as robotics and phones. Quality is roughly equal to the autoregressive model there, so the latency advantage decides it. ([25:09](https://youtu.be/r305-aQTaU0?t=1509))

## Techniques
- **Discrete corruption and denoising training** — Take a clean token sequence, corrupt it at many noise levels (random token insertion or replacement), and train a network to predict the clean tokens. Sampling starts from random vocabulary tokens and repeats refinement steps until the sequence settles. Continuous or latent diffusion is possible but he says most text diffusion work is discrete today. *When:* When you want non-autoregressive text generation and can afford several full forward passes per block of output.
- **Latency-first serving by block** — Generate a fixed-size block (he mentions 32, 512 or 1000 tokens) with diffusion refinement, then move to the next block. For unbounded length, use blockwise autoregression over diffusion windows, or add a head that predicts response length so generation can stop early. *When:* When a single user needs very fast responses and throughput across many users matters less, such as on-device agents.
- **Test-time compute via denoising steps** — Treat the number of refinement passes as a budget. More passes usually raise quality on evals, and easy prompts finish early because the model stops when it judges the output done. *When:* When you want a quality/latency dial per request without changing the model.
- **Self-correction through reason-then-revise** — Let the model write its reasoning and answer in one canvas. Later passes can revise earlier tokens (for example a wrong final answer) once the full reasoning is visible, with no separate critique step. *When:* For short arithmetic or logic problems where a left-to-right model commits early to a wrong answer.
- **In-place infilling and editing** — Keep the unchanged context in the canvas, mask the span to change, and refine only that span. He demonstrates bug fixes, docstring insertion and mid-story paragraph insertion. *When:* For edits to existing code or text where regenerating the whole output would be wasteful or would change unrelated parts.

## Hot takes
- Speaker is a DeepMind research scientist presenting an unreleased team direction. The talk is promotional for their work and cites no paper; the year-old Gemini Diffusion numbers are demo-scale and not reproducible from the talk.
- The self-correction claim rests on one anecdotal square-root prompt, compared against two other models in a live demo. Treat it as a motivating example, not a measured property.
- The claim that larger diffusion models need fewer denoising steps, so serving costs fall with scale, is asserted without data. He admits he does not know the serving-cost trend at that scale.
- The latency case is hardware-based: for a single user, fewer weight-streaming passes should win. The throughput loss at large batch is the reason the speaker gives for diffusion not being used in big served models today, which he presents as a real limitation rather than a fixable detail.
- The 15-second voice-coded to-do app and the generated Wikipedia, Reddit and OS demos are showcase material. They show what low latency allows, not evidence of quality or reliability.
- Quality and speed are only compared to a single older autoregressive baseline. He says quality is 'basically the same,' with no benchmark table.
