> Sebastian Fox (Composo) on why LLM judges miss serious clinical-note errors: "which error matters" is tacit and shifting, so keep it as retrieved, case-specific expert judgments and run a discover-capture-calibrate loop.

## Summary
Fox, a medical doctor who runs Composo, argues that AI-written clinical notes from production ambient scribes contain serious errors that look fine on the page: added facts, changed meanings, and dropped key lines. He says most of these errors sit where automated checks do not catch them. The hard part is not spotting a difference between transcript and note but judging which differences matter, and that standard is tacit, contextual and moving. He reports that even a strong frontier-model judge with a detailed faithfulness rubric passed about one in five clean-looking notes that had a serious error. He argues against freezing the standard in a prompt/rubric or in model weights, and proposes keeping it as examples: discover failure modes from real outputs, capture expert comments (free text, not just scores), and for each output retrieve similar past judged cases, corrections and guidelines into the judge's context. He says this loop beat the rubric-based judges on the same notes, and that the gain came from what the judge was shown, not from more compute or a better prompt. The talk is a practitioner argument with some internal data, and its generalisation to other high-stakes domains is his claim.

## Key ideas
- **The dangerous failures look fine** — The opening example is a clinical note that reads as routine. The missing line (jaw pain on chewing, in a new headache over 50) is what changes the risk. Omissions are the hard case because nothing on the page is wrong. ([0:08](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=8))
- **Most errors escape automated checks** — Across three production ambient scribes, the speaker plotted every failure from a week of generated notes by severity and by whether a strong automated check catches it. Only a handful of high-severity errors are caught; most high-stakes ones sit in the uncaught region. ([3:18](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=198))
- **Errors come from generation, not only transcription** — Even with a perfect transcript, the model adds something not said, changes something said, or omits something that should be there. The blatant form is easy to catch; the hard part is deciding whether the change matters. ([5:21](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=321))
- **The 'which matters' call is tacit, contextual and moving** — Experts hold this judgment but cannot fully write it down. The same detail matters in one case and not another (e.g., a travel history: France vs Lake Malawi). Guidelines change and good clinicians disagree, so there is no fixed target to encode. ([6:22](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=382))
- **A strong rubric-based judge still lets serious errors through** — He ran the same notes through a serious judge (frontier model, detailed faithfulness rubric with worked examples, auto-optimised rubric, deterministic concept counts). It flagged some errors but about one in five of its clean passes still had a serious error, often an omission. A weak judge adds a second silent failure that agrees with the first. ([8:23](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=503))
- **Keep the standard as examples, not in the prompt or weights** — He lists three homes for taste: the prompt (a fixed rubric), the weights (fine-tuning, which goes stale and cannot explain itself), or the examples themselves. He argues for examples: past judgments, expert corrections and references, retrieved per output and live on the next call. ([12:49](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=769))
- **Run a discover-capture-calibrate loop** — Discover failure modes by clustering real production outputs and naming them, not by guessing on a whiteboard. Capture expert judgment as free-text reasoning and corrections, not just scores (a clinician spent a few focused hours). Calibrate each output against the judged cases most similar to it, then loop back when new modes or a moving standard appear. ([14:15](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=855))
- **Assemble a case-specific standard per output** — The judge gets the nearest judged cases and their scores, the applicable expert corrections, and the reference guidelines. Generic criteria can be written once; what counts as a serious miss for this note is assembled on the fly. He calls this context engineering. ([15:42](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=942))
- **The same notes score better when the judge is shown more** — On one dataset, a strong off-the-shelf judge was better than a coin flip but missed most of what mattered. The rubric-plus-optimiser system did better. The loop-based judge did best. He attributes the gap to what the judge was shown, not to more compute or a better prompt. ([16:44](https://youtu.be/yqF6XhzbWBk?t=1004))

## Techniques
- **Failure-mode discovery from production outputs** — Run the system on real traffic, cluster what goes wrong, and name the clusters to form a failure-mode ontology. Use the ontology to decide what to ask experts about and how to index cases. Do not rely on synthetic cases, which only cover failures already imagined. *When:* Before writing any judge rubric, and again whenever a new failure type shows up in production.
- **Free-text expert comments as the raw eval data** — Put real outputs in front of domain experts for a short focused session and collect their reasoning and corrections, not only a pass/fail or score. Store these as the labelled record the judge will retrieve later. *When:* At the start of an eval program, when there are no labelled data and experts are scarce. He suggests this is the easiest first step.
- **Retrieval of similar judged cases into the judge context** — For each output, retrieve the most similar previously judged outputs with their scores, the applicable expert corrections and the relevant guidelines, and put them in the judge's context. Adding a new judgment changes the next call's standard, with no retraining. The score can be traced back to the examples that moved it. *When:* When the standard for 'good' moves over time, is contextual, and must be explainable, such as in clinical, legal or contract review.
- **Generate-then-check with a narrower checker** — Let a cheap generator produce output, then run a checker that does one narrow job (find the one thing that is wrong). Pass only output that clears the bar. Give the checker more tokens and the specific failure modes to hunt for. *When:* When generation is cheap and verification is easier to specify than generation. He notes this asymmetry works for maths and code, but that the value judgment part does not reduce to it.
- **Severity vs. detectability map of errors** — Plot each observed error on two axes: how much it matters and whether an automated check catches it. Errors in the high-severity, not-caught region show where the eval has a blind spot. *When:* To audit an existing automated eval or guardrail, to show where it is failing, and to prioritise what to capture next.

## Hot takes
- A rubric-based LLM judge can add a second silent failure: it confidently passes errors the generator made.
- The standard of 'good' for high-stakes output cannot be written down in advance; freezing it in a prompt or in weights makes it go stale.
- Keeping evaluation taste as retrieved, case-specific examples is both better and cheaper than rubric tuning or fine-tuning.
- Evaluation is not something you build once and freeze; it is continuous work.
