> Short Better Stack explainer on attackers hiding malware and prompt-injection text in DNS TXT records, why encrypted DNS makes this harder to see, and what DNS monitoring to add.

## Summary
The speaker explains that DNS TXT records can carry arbitrary text, which makes them a place to hide payloads even though they are normally used for harmless things like domain verification. He traces the idea to a researcher who stored images across TXT records and to a security group that found a 2021 attempt that split a payload across numbered subdomains. He gives a simplified attack model: hex-encode a file, split it into hundreds of chunks, place each chunk in a TXT record on an attacker-owned subdomain, then have a compromised host query and reassemble them. He argues the problem is getting worse because encrypted DNS (DoH/DoT) hides lookups from security tools, and because TXT records are now being used to carry prompt-injection strings aimed at AI chatbots. His defensive advice is to watch DNS for anomalies such as one device making hundreds of TXT lookups to strange subdomains, run your own resolver for visibility, ingest those logs, and block known-bad domains with current threat-intel lists. The video is brief and mostly conceptual, with no measured data or tested detection rules.

## Key ideas
- **DNS TXT records accept arbitrary text, so they can be used as a hiding place for payloads even though they normally hold harmless verification data.** — The speaker frames TXT records as the key enabler: the record type is trusted and rarely inspected, so it works as a storage channel. ([0:00](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=0))
- **The technique has precedent. A researcher stored images across TXT records and a later group found a 2021 instance that spread chunks across subdomains.** — Historical lookups with DNSDB Scout surfaced the 2021 case. The speaker says this is not a new technique and that researchers have known about DNS delivery for years. ([1:10](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=70))
- **The attack needs no download or email attachment. Chunks are fetched and reassembled entirely through ordinary DNS lookups.** — Attacker side: hex-encode the file, split into hundreds of chunks, one per TXT record on numbered subdomains. Compromised host side: a script queries the subdomains, orders the chunks, and rebuilds the binary. ([2:06](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=126))
- **Encrypted DNS (DNS over HTTPS or DNS over TLS) makes this harder to see because security tools can no longer read which domains are requested.** — The speaker presents encryption as the trend that will make detection harder over time, and he returns to this in the defenses section. ([2:06](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=126))
- **TXT records are now also used to deliver prompt-injection strings aimed at AI chatbots, for example instructions like 'ignore all previous instructions' hidden in record text.** — Examples shown include a request to summarize a movie, a request to output ROT13, and a role-play instruction. Any agent that reads DNS-derived text is exposed. ([3:12](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=192))
- **The practical defense is to watch DNS as a signal source: alert on TXT query volume per device and unfamiliar subdomains, and keep your own resolver and logs.** — The example anomaly is one device making hundreds of TXT lookups to strange subdomains. Running an internal resolver restores visibility once traffic is encrypted, and log ingestion lets you search for these patterns. ([3:12](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=192))
- **Blocking known-malicious domains with up-to-date threat-intel lists is still a basic control.** — Stated as the final defense, with no details on which feeds or tooling. ([4:14](https://youtu.be/66EG2a81_rE?t=254))

## Techniques
- **Alert on DNS TXT query volume per host** — Log DNS queries and filter by record type TXT. Alert when one device makes a large number of TXT lookups (the speaker's example is hundreds) to unfamiliar or algorithmically named subdomains of a single domain. Treat the per-host, per-parent-domain count as the signal. *When:* Any organization with DNS query logs that can see internal hosts. Most useful for catching chunked exfiltration or C2 that uses TXT records.
- **Run your own DNS resolver for visibility** — Point internal hosts at an internal recursive resolver so their lookups stay visible even when clients use encrypted DNS to external resolvers. Block or deny encrypted DNS to outside resolvers where policy allows, so lookups go through the monitored path. *When:* Networks where DoH/DoT is spreading and security tools can no longer see domain names. The speaker recommends this specifically as encryption becomes more common.
- **Ship resolver logs to a log platform and search for patterns** — Ingest resolver query logs into a log store (the speaker names Better Stack) and write saved searches for the TXT-volume and odd-subdomain patterns above. *When:* After running an internal resolver. Turns the resolver from a passive control into a detection source.
- **Block known-bad domains with threat-intel lists** — Keep blocklists of known malicious domains current and enforce them at the resolver or firewall. *When:* As a baseline control. The speaker does not describe feed choice or update cadence.

## Hot takes
- The 'nightmare scenarios' framing is hype. The speaker himself says the technique is not new and that researchers have known about DNS delivery for years.
- The title says hackers 'are now' hiding malware this way, but the examples cited date back to 2021 and a 2020-era proof of concept, so the trend claim rests on a few anecdotes.
- The defensive advice is generic. There is no detection rule, no thresholds validated against normal traffic, and no discussion of false positives from legitimate TXT users such as SPF, DKIM and ACME validation.
- A product plug for Better Stack log ingestion appears inside the defenses section at 4:14. It is a brief mention, not a sponsored segment, but the recommendation is tied to the vendor.
