> Adam Nahari (Apollo) argues AI pilots fail to move EBIT because most spend goes to general tools and change management, and sets out six moves (pick one or two P&L-linked problems, redesign the process end to end, get CEO sponsorship, build with the business, put tools in everyone's hands, pre-set kill metrics) to make AI show up in margin.

## Summary
Adam Nahari opens with an airline crew-scheduling desk that re-crews a flight in 15 minutes by hand, with crew as the second-largest cost after fuel. He contrasts broad adoption (about 90% of businesses use AI in some function) with the small share (about 6%) reporting significant EBIT impact, and argues that the gap is mostly change management rather than engineering, by his estimate roughly five units of change management to one of engineering. He sorts AI into three layers: general productivity tools whose gains get competed away into price, domain copilots, and core operational AI embedded in business processes, where gains stay in margin. His six moves are: choose one or two problems tied to a P&L driver, redesign the workflow end to end (his airline example contrasts a 10% assistive tool with a 10x optimization engine), get the CEO publicly committed, build with the P&L owner and embedded domain champions, get every user experimenting with the tools, and set kill metrics (90-day time gate, A/B metric gate, adoption gate above 50%) with the CFO before starting. The talk ends with a unit-economics test: at-scale cost including compute, tokens and people must be well below the impact. The evidence is one airline case and his own experience, with no outcome data across companies.

## Key ideas
- **Broad AI adoption is not showing up in P&L: about 90% of businesses use AI somewhere, but only about 6% report significant EBIT impact, and time savings (about 2% of US working hours) mostly do not reach the P&L.** — Speaker compares this to the 1980s computer-productivity paradox. Gains from general tools are competed away into lower prices instead of higher margin. ([1:07](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=67))
- **Change management, not engineering, is the bottleneck: his rule of thumb is about five units of change management per unit of engineering, and the engineering share keeps shrinking as models improve.** — Picking the problem, getting buy-in, driving adoption and measuring impact is where time goes and where failures happen. ([2:33](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=153))
- **Three layers of AI value: general productivity (copilots, chatbots), domain-specific tools (function copilots, retrieval over business documents), and core operational AI embedded in business processes. Only the third layer's gains tend to stay in margin, because it cannot be bought off the shelf.** — Businesses with material EBIT impact are about 3x as likely to redesign a workflow end to end rather than just adopting a tool, and they invest in layer three on top of layer one. ([3:35](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=215))
- **Move 1: pick one or two problems, not 40, by working backward from a P&L north-star metric through its drivers. Use-case screen: a line the CFO recognizes, a baseline agreed before the pilot, data infrastructure ready and owned by the company, a named individual champion, and a CEO top-five priority.** — For an airline the drivers were routes (demand), tails (supply), maintenance (constraints) and crew (cost). Crew was chosen as the second-largest cost after fuel. ([4:58](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=298))
- **Move 2: redesign the whole process around the new technology. Incremental automation gives incremental gains; the 10x version was an optimization engine that enumerates all legal crew re-assignments and ranks them by cost in under a minute.** — The project also consolidated flight, crew and maintenance data and built a decision-support app. Getting schedulers to use it was the hard part, not the engineering. The 10%/10x figures are illustrative. ([8:04](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=484))
- **Move 3: no funding without visible CEO commitment. Without it, data requests take weeks and scheduling conflicts pile up, and the alignment decays over time, so it has to be renewed.** — He says the correlation with success is strong enough that he will not fund an initiative without a public CEO commitment. ([8:04](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=484))
- **Move 4: build with the business. The P&L owner (VP of crew scheduling) co-sponsors, and embedded champion schedulers join design from day one, meet engineers every other day, co-design the interface, take part in A/B tests, and then persuade their peers.** — He recruited a CIO, a VP of data and AI, and a senior data-science director with both technical and airline domain expertise. He concludes that organizational investment exceeded technology investment. ([9:32](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=572))
- **Move 5: put the tools in everyone's hands. Three of four people in a 30-executive workshop had never used an AI tool before that week, and redesigning a workflow with AI is impossible without hands-on use.** — Experimenting with the tools is presented as the minimum requirement for finding where value lies. ([10:54](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=654))
- **Move 6: write the kill metrics before starting a pilot and let the CFO keep score. Three gates: a time gate (90 days per stage), a metric gate (A/B against a baseline agreed in advance), and an adoption gate (over 50% of crew schedulers using the tool).** — Final test with the CFO: the at-scale cost, including compute, tokens and the people involved, must be well below the impact. He says many pilots die at this step. ([12:16](https://youtu.be/vUJDyDnqT4c?t=736))

## Techniques
- **Work-backward use-case screen** — Start from a P&L north-star metric and break it into drivers. Keep a use case only if it passes five checks: a line the CFO recognizes, a measurable baseline agreed before the pilot, data infrastructure ready and company-owned, a named individual user as champion, and a CEO top-five priority. Limit to one or two problems. *When:* Choosing which AI initiative to fund first, especially for core operational workflows that cannot be bought off the shelf.
- **End-to-end workflow redesign over assistive tooling** — Do not just bolt a chatbot onto the existing process. Consolidate the relevant data, build an optimization engine that enumerates every feasible option under the real constraints and ranks them by cost, and redesign the workflow and the user's decision support around it. *When:* Constraint-heavy operational decisions (scheduling, routing, allocation) where many legal options exist and a human currently picks one under time pressure.
- **Embedded domain champions in the build loop** — Put frontline users who are respected by peers into design from day one. Have them meet the engineering team on a regular cadence, co-design the interface, take part in A/B tests, and act as peer advocates for rollout. Recruit leaders with both technical and domain knowledge. *When:* Any tool meant to change how a specific team makes decisions; adoption is driven by peers, not by mandate.
- **Pre-registered kill gates** — Before the pilot starts, write down three gates with the CFO: a time gate (for example 90 days per stage, stop if no value is shown), a metric gate (A/B testing against a baseline fixed in advance), and an adoption gate (for example, more than 50% of target users actively using the tool). Stop the initiative if a gate fails. *When:* Before committing budget to any AI pilot, to prevent open-ended pilots that never get cancelled.
- **At-scale unit economics check** — Estimate the cost of running the initiative at full scale, including compute, token spend and the staff needed to run and maintain it, and compare that to the expected financial impact. Proceed only if the impact clearly exceeds the total cost. *When:* Before scaling a pilot beyond its first stage, with finance involved in the decision.

## Hot takes
- Strategy talk with no code, no architecture and no outcome data: the six moves are one practitioner's framework drawn mainly from one airline crew-scheduling case.
- The 5:1 change-management-to-engineering ratio and the 3x-more-likely redesign finding are presented without a source or sample; treat them as the speaker's mental model.
- The 90%/6%/2% adoption statistics are cited without sources in the talk.
- The 10% vs 10x numbers are explicitly illustrative, not measured results.
- Useful portable idea: kill gates and the CFO unit-economics check, which apply to any internal AI pilot, not only airlines.
- Caption error: the 1987 productivity quote is attributed to 'Robert Zalo' in the captions, presumably Robert Solow.
