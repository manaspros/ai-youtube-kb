> Tejas Kumar (IBM) walks through evals as fuzzy unit tests for non-deterministic agents, then builds an LLM judge live, calibrates it against hand-labelled data, and gates CI on human agreement.

## Summary
The speaker frames evals as ahead-of-time reliability that complements harnesses, which give just-in-time reliability. An eval is a set of test cases with expected behavior, a judge that gives a verdict, and an aggregate score, and he calls them fuzzy unit tests. He lists the judge biases that can make evals lie: position bias, sycophancy, self-preference, and verbosity bias. The core method is calibrating an LLM judge on a hand-labelled set of about 30 examples until it agrees with humans roughly 80 to 85 percent of the time, with 100 percent treated as a sign of overfit or bias. He then demonstrates the calibration loop live: each disagreement points to a missing policy fact, which is injected into the judge prompt until the agreement passes the threshold. He recommends running the judge as a CI agreement gate, pinning the judge model to a dated version, and keeping the dataset alive by sampling production traffic and human-scoring it. The talk ends with a cheapest-first eval ladder and a note that the judge should see the same policy and retrieval as the production agent. A good part of the talk covers IBM's OpenRAG product, which the speaker presents as the policy retriever.

## Key ideas
- **Evals and harnesses are two complementary reliability layers** — A harness gives just-in-time reliability while the agent is running. Evals give ahead-of-time reliability by testing before shipping. The speaker calls them the two pedals of a bicycle and argues that a good harness plus good evals lets cheaper models go far. ([1:21](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=81))
- **Evals are reliability tests for non-deterministic systems** — Unit tests assert an exact output for a deterministic function. Evals ask whether the agent behaved acceptably across many scenarios. Every eval set has test cases with inputs, expected behavior described in direction rather than exact strings, a judge, and an aggregation into a score. ([9:43](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=583))
- **Pointwise judging is more reliable than pairwise judging** — The speaker argues that people, and LLM judges modeled on them, struggle to pick between many options. Pass/fail for one answer is easier and more stable. He cites a study on pairwise weakness, though the exact figure is garbled in the transcript. ([16:34](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=994))
- **LLM judges have four named biases that make evals lie** — Position bias favors whichever option comes first, which can be checked by swapping the order. Sycophancy favors the most pleasing answer, even when it breaks policy. Self-preference favors text from the judge's own model family, so the judge should come from a different family. Verbosity bias favors longer answers. ([17:52](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=1072))
- **Calibrate the judge against a hand-labelled golden set, targeting 80 to 85 percent agreement** — Write about 30 input and answer pairs with human verdicts, then hide the verdicts and have the judge score each one. Compare the agreement. Around 80 percent matches human-to-human agreement. 100 percent suggests overfitting or a sycophantic or biased judge. ([21:33](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=1293))
- **Debug judge disagreements as missing knowledge, not as model failure** — For each disagreement, find the fact the judge did not have, usually a policy clause, and add it to the prompt. The speaker went from many failures to 3 out of 30 this way, then switched to a stronger model and added an explicit rule that policy is non-negotiable. ([40:12](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=2412))
- **Put the judge in CI as an agreement gate, and keep the dataset alive** — The CI test fails if judge-human agreement drops below the threshold, and the test also prints the agreement percentage. Pin the judge to a dated model snapshot, not an alias. In production, sample real traffic, score it by hand, add it to the set, and re-check agreement. ([24:00](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=1440))
- **Use the cheapest possible check first, and climb the eval ladder only as needed** — Start with deterministic unit tests. If that is not enough, use regex or pattern matching, then validate traces such as tool call counts and durations, and only then use an LLM judge, starting with the cheapest model. ([52:25](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=3145))
- **The judge should share the production agent's policy and retrieval** — Policy should be a single source, queried by both the production agent and the judge. The judge should mirror the production agent, including its retrieval tools, so that a CI failure predicts a production failure. ([57:54](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=3474))

## Techniques
- **Judge calibration loop against a golden set** — Hand-label about 30 cases (input, answer, human verdict). Remove the labels, run the judge, and compute agreement. For each disagreement, identify the missing fact, add it to the judge prompt, and re-run until agreement falls in the 80 to 85 percent band. *When:* Before using any LLM judge as a gate or metric, and whenever the judge's verdicts drift from human judgment.
- **Pointwise pass/fail judging** — Ask the judge for one answer at a time, returning only pass or fail against a policy or rubric, instead of ranking two options. *When:* Default choice for production evals. Use pairwise only if you add order-swapping checks.
- **Bias probes for judges** — Swap the order of options to test position bias. Have a judge from a different model family grade outputs from the generator to test self-preference. Plant polite but wrong answers to test sycophancy, and wordy but wrong answers to test verbosity bias. *When:* When setting up a judge, and again after any model change.
- **CI agreement gate** — Write the eval as a normal test runner test. It logs the judge-human agreement percentage, passes when agreement is above a lower bound (about 80 percent), and fails when agreement is below it. The speaker also suggests an upper bound to flag overfitting at 100 percent. *When:* To block agent or prompt changes that make the judge disagree with humans.
- **Pin the judge model version** — Use a dated snapshot identifier for the judge instead of a rolling alias, so an upstream change cannot silently break the eval. *When:* Any time an LLM judge runs in CI or production.
- **Living eval dataset from production traffic** — Sample real user inputs and agent outputs, have humans score them, add the scored cases to the dataset, and re-check judge agreement on the new data. *When:* After launch, to keep evals current as user behavior and policy change.
- **Cheapest-first eval ladder** — Run exact match and schema validation first, then regex, then trace checks on tool calls and latency, and only then an LLM judge, starting with the smallest model that passes calibration. *When:* Designing an eval pipeline with cost in mind.
- **Adversarial and synthetic eval generation** — Use agents to generate new test cases from known failures, or red-team prompts that try to break the system, and add them to the eval set. *When:* When real traffic is too thin to cover failure modes.
- **Avoid fuzzy string matching on LLM output** — The speaker's example: a test that checks whether the answer contains the word cannot passed a wrong answer that contained the word. Use a judge or structured tool-call checks instead. *When:* Whenever an assertion is applied to free-text model output.

## Numbers
- Hand-labelled judge calibration set of about 30 examples. Target judge-human agreement is 80 to 85 percent. Around 80 percent is also the typical human-to-human agreement the speaker cites. ([22:47](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=1367))
- First live run of the GPT-3.5 Turbo judge on 30 examples was over the failure ceiling (more than 5 failures). After lowercasing the comparison, it was near the threshold. ([38:57](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=2337))
- After adding the policy to the prompt, 6 of 30 failed. After adding more policy and upgrading to GPT-4o-mini, 3 of 30 failed, which is 27 of 30 (90 percent) agreement. ([45:05](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=2705))
- Speaker-cited Instagram AI support chatbot incident: about 20,225 accounts compromised, after attackers used a VPN to bypass 2FA and got the bot to add a secondary email. Speaker's figure, not verified. ([6:06](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=366))
- Speaker-cited study: pairwise comparison is about 39 percent more vulnerable to attack than pointwise. The transcript wording is garbled and this figure is unverified. ([17:52](https://youtu.be/NdrSPm6NCdk?t=1072))

## Hot takes
- Evals are essentially fuzzy unit tests, and the only deterministic thing about AI systems is that nothing is deterministic.
- Do not write evals for skills you wrote yourself, since skills are deterministic from your side. Evals belong to whoever builds or ships the harness.
- A judge agreeing with humans 100 percent of the time is a red flag, not a success.
- Judges should be from a different model family than the generator, because same-family judges pick their own output.
