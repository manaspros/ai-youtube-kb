# Open Source AI is dying (and I know why)

> Kai argues open-weight AI died at the size where models no longer fit on hardware you own. Below that line the freedom holds, and frontier open weights now work mainly as a price ceiling on hosted APIs.

## Summary
The speaker opens with the 2023-2024 excitement around running open models locally and says that dream no longer holds at the frontier. He uses Kimi K3 (594 GB, over a million tokens of context) to show that open weights can be downloaded yet not run without a data center. He then works through the rental cost of 8 B200 GPUs (about $30k/month on Lambda, about $82k/month on AWS) against hosted per-token prices, and a DeepSeek V4 Flash run on a 192 GB M5 Max that tops out around 64k to 100k usable tokens. Hosted routers add their own risks: the same model name can come from providers at different quantization levels, with different output caps and silently reduced context windows. He argues the weights are a commoditized complement to cloud serving, and that license clauses (revenue caps, territory exclusions) now bite at exactly the sizes nobody can serve locally. Small open models such as Gemma 4 31B and Qwen 3.6 still work fine on consumer GPUs, and local remains the right choice for privacy, offline use and reproducibility. He closes by telling viewers to check file size, license and which provider the router picked.

## Key ideas
- **The original open-source promise (download weights, run them on hardware you own, control them) held for roughly 2023 to 2024, when good open models were 7B to 34B and fit consumer hardware.** — The speaker says the frontier has moved past what local hardware can hold. What died is the ownership and control dream at frontier scale; open weights still work at smaller sizes. ([1:24](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=84))
- **Downloadable is not the same as runnable. Kimi K3 is published with open weights under a permissive license, but its 594 GB file needs roughly eight 80 GB H100s just to hold the weights, before any KV cache for its million-token context.** — He calls this freedom that requires a data center to exercise, which he distinguishes from the 2023 meaning of the word. ([2:49](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=169))
- **Open weights still matter as a market force. Open weights act as a price ceiling on closed APIs, which the speaker calls price regulation performed by a file nobody can run, and that is distinct from software freedom.** — He cites Chinese open-weight models at about 61% of OpenRouter tokens by May, and a GPT-4-class token price falling from $20 per million in late 2022 to about $0.40 by the end of 2025. ([5:08](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=308))
- **Self-hosting a frontier model only pays off at very high utilization. The node bills while idle, while the API bills per token.** — Eight B200s on Lambda at $42.32 per hour break even against Kimi K3 output pricing at about 2.8M output tokens per hour, which is about 784 tokens per second sustained, around the clock, and several hundred concurrent conversations at 20 to 40 tokens per second each. ([6:26](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=386))
- **Local context is much smaller than the advertised window. Weights consume most of the memory, and the remaining KV-cache budget sets the real limit.** — DeepSeek V4 Flash at 4-bit takes about 150 GB on a 192 GB M5 Max, leaving about 30 GB for KV cache, which is roughly 64k to 100k tokens. Past about 200k tokens, macOS swaps KV cache to SSD and throughput collapses. ([7:13](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=433))
- **A model name on a router is not a spec. The same model can be served at different precisions and with different limits, and the router defaults to the cheapest option.** — Llama 3.3 70B is served by 13 providers at BF16, FP16, FP8 and unspecified quantization, with output prices from $0.32 to $2.25 per million. DeepSeek V3 at FP4 caps output at 16,384 tokens, while BF16 caps it at 163,840. ([8:39](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=519))
- **Provider context caps can be silently lower than advertised. The agent does not error; it compacts and drops the middle of the conversation, then bills for rereading the rest.** — He cites a Reddit report of a 1M-token model served at a 32k cap on DigitalOcean, and DeepSeek V4 Pro advertised at 87k instead of 1M. He says the agent just compacts and bills the extra rereading. ([9:51](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=591))
- **Labs publish weights as a complement to their cloud product, following Joel Spolsky's 2002 'commoditize your complement' idea.** — The speaker says weights are the complement and cloud serving is the product. He cites Alibaba AI cloud revenue up 45% last quarter, and a 30B Meta open model with 665k downloads against 52k for Alibaba's 2.4T flagship. ([11:11](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=671))
- **Licenses tightened in the same direction this summer. The clauses trigger at sizes that need 8 to 64 accelerators, so they bite where local serving is already impossible.** — Kimi K3 license restricts providers above $20M revenue, Alibaba's model has a similar clause at $50M, and Minimax excludes the US, EU, UK and South Korea from its territory. Meta stopped publishing open weights after April 2025 and shut its model API in July. ([12:30](https://youtu.be/zxnXemN98d0?t=750))

## Techniques
- **Break-even check for self-hosting vs API** — Divide the node's hourly cost by the API's output price per token to get tokens per hour (for example $42.32/hr over $15 per million gives about 2.8M tokens/hr, or 784 tokens/s). Then divide by per-conversation throughput (20 to 40 tokens/s) to get the number of concurrent conversations needed to break even. *When:* Deciding whether to self-host a large open-weight model or pay per token for a hosted API.
- **Local memory budget before choosing a model** — Take the quantized weight size (about 150 GB for DeepSeek V4 Flash at 4-bit), subtract OS and overhead from total unified memory (192 GB on an M5 Max), and treat the remainder as KV-cache budget. Convert that into tokens, which gives roughly 64k to 100k for this case. Keep working context under the point where swapping starts (about 200k here). *When:* Sizing a local deployment against the advertised context window, especially on unified-memory Macs.
- **Pin the provider, not just the model name** — When calling a routed open model, check each provider's quantization level, max output tokens and context cap. Pin a specific provider instead of accepting the router's cheapest default, so the same model ID means the same serving configuration. *When:* Using a router such as OpenRouter for open-weight models where behavior varies by provider.
- **Detect silent compaction and context caps** — Track input token counts per call and watch for unexplained drops in conversation content or repeated reads. A quiet compaction is the symptom to look for, as the speaker describes, because the agent does not error when a provider's effective cap is lower than advertised. *When:* Running long agent sessions on hosted open-weight models, where the advertised context may not match the provider's real limit.
- **Pre-adoption license and file audit** — Before adopting an open-weight release, check the file size against your GPU memory, read the license for revenue thresholds and territory exclusions, and confirm which provider the router will actually use. *When:* Evaluating a new open-weight model for production or for a commercial product.

## Hot takes
- The headline 'open source AI is dying' is rhetorical. The body concludes that open weights still work below a size threshold, and the 'died' claim is scoped to frontier-size models.
- Several market figures (OpenRouter 61% share, download counts, the Alibaba and Meta comparisons) are cited without sources in the transcript and should be verified before reuse.
- Provider-variance and silent-compaction points (quantization spread, max_output and context caps) are the most practically useful content, and they are backed by specific, checkable examples.
- The 'commoditize your complement' framing treats open weights as a strategic loss-leader; the speaker presents it as the reason labs release weights, not as a claim about their intent.
