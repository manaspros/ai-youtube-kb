> Short pitch-craft talk: explain an AI product by opening with the user's pain, tying it to a picturable story, and showing a concrete before/after.

## Summary
Veronica Hylak argues that AI products fail when buyers cannot explain them after a short pitch, and that the usual jargon-heavy pitch ("agentic orchestration platform") makes people feel nothing. She proposes a three-part fix: name the wound (the user's daily pain, shown through a human moment), make the product click (a picturable analogy such as a 17-year-old test or a viral AI mistake story), and show the transformation (a before and after with concrete time savings). She suggests a roughly 20-second structure: wound, "we fixed that", then how. She also advises banning abstract phrases that no one can picture and replacing them with a mental image, while saying the technical definition can come later. The talk is short and mostly about messaging, with one worked before/after example and no engineering detail.

## Key ideas
- **Typical AI pitches fail because buyers feel nothing and cannot repeat what the product does.** — She contrasts jargon like 'agentic orchestration platform' with a human-centred opening, and says the window to make a mark is about one elevator ride. ([0:00](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=0))
- **Start with the wound, not with what you built.** — The first slide should show the user's daily pain (the moment they want to throw the laptop out the window). Her example: security teams juggling alerts, tickets and vulnerabilities across disconnected tools. ([0:54](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=54))
- **The pitch format is wound, then 'we fixed that', then how, all in about 20 seconds.** — Once the pain is shown, the product only needs to click once the listener can see how it changes their day. ([2:15](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=135))
- **Use a 17-year-old test to check whether the product is understandable.** — If a teenager could not say what the product does, the room will probably be lost. Tie the product to a story people already know, such as the McDonald's AI drive-through clips, instead of opening with a category label. ([2:15](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=135))
- **Ban vague words and replace them with a mental image.** — Examples given: 'Devin, the AI software engineer' creates a picture; 'a smoke alarm for AI behavior' does too. Technical accuracy is not required at this stage, since these are entry points to the deeper conversation. ([3:29](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=209))
- **Show the transformation as a concrete before and after.** — Statements like 'we improve code quality' or 'we increase productivity' do not tell the buyer what changes. Her example: support digging through docs for 30 minutes versus asking one question and getting a sourced answer in 10 seconds. ([3:29](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=209))
- **Re-running the elevator pitch with the same product can produce a much clearer story.** — In her rewrite, the old world (hours spent searching Slack, email and spreadsheets) and the new world (one search, answer in seconds) are shown together. The product is the same; only the story changes. ([4:41](https://youtu.be/d_Ftrl3vfV0?t=281))

## Techniques
- **Wound-first opening** — Open the pitch or page with one specific, human moment of pain in the buyer's day (for example, tools scattered across systems), then state the fix. Do not begin with the product category. *When:* First slide of a pitch, landing page hero, or any explanation of an AI product to a non-technical buyer.
- **Viral-story anchor** — Link the product to a story the audience already knows (her example: an AI drive-through making odd orders) so the product's purpose is obvious at once. Run the 17-year-old test: could a teenager explain what this does? *When:* When the product category is new or abstract and the audience has no shared vocabulary.
- **Picturable-phrase rule** — Scan copy for phrases no one can visualise, such as 'agentic orchestration' or 'autonomous intelligence', and replace each with an image (a named analogy or a physical metaphor). Save the precise technical definition for later. *When:* Writing taglines, pitch decks, or docs intended for buyers before a technical deep dive.
- **Before/after transformation with numbers** — Describe the old workflow and the new one side by side, with a time measure on each (for example, 30 minutes vs 10 seconds) and the source attached, so the buyer can feel the change. *When:* Proving value to a buyer or stakeholder after the problem has been introduced.

## Hot takes
- Great tech that nobody understands dies quietly.
- Do not start a pitch with what you built; start with the buyer's pain.
- Technical definitions can wait; a picturable image is the first thing buyers need.
