> A MongoDB developer advocate walks through a four-phase framework (product requirements, system design, evaluation and monitoring, cost/latency optimization) using a health insurance claims review system as the worked example.

## Summary
The speaker, Apoorva Joshi of MongoDB, argues that AI-generated code makes product specs more important, not less, and lays out a four-phase framework for building AI systems. The worked example is a health insurance claims review tool that assists medical reviewers. Requirements come first: a quantified, solution-agnostic business problem, business constraints (data residency, approved models, mandatory human review for denials and complex cases), the role of AI (complementary, reactive, semi-autonomous), and SMART success metrics. System design covers data sources, update cadence, retrieval method per source, the simplest architecture that fits (RAG plus a controlled workflow with human-in-the-loop), and UX and feedback. Evaluation covers guardrails on inputs and outputs, offline quality metrics, and production monitoring such as human override rate. Optimization covers accuracy levers (prompting, reranking, memory), cost and latency levers (semantic caching, batching), and reliability through structured outputs. The talk is a practitioner walkthrough with no benchmarks, and most of its content is general design guidance.

## Key ideas
- **Specs are the new code. Vibe coding is fine for low-stakes work, but anything other people depend on needs explicit requirements, system design, and evaluation criteria first.** — She cites Anthropic and OpenAI speakers saying the same thing. Her framework has four phases: product requirements, system design, evaluation and monitoring, and cost/latency/reliability optimization. ([1:19](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=79))
- **Write the business problem so it names a specific user and a measured baseline, and leave out the solution.** — Her example says reviewers take two days per claim, which is 4x the standard for non-urgent cases and 12x for urgent ones. It does not say whether the system is an agent or a workflow. That decision comes later. ([3:49](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=229))
- **Collect constraints early, before any design work: compliance, data residency, approved vendors, human-review rules, latency, monthly LLM budget, and uptime.** — In her example, patient data must stay in the approved cloud, only approved models can be used, and denials and complex cases need human review. These limits decide what can be automated. ([5:11](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=311))
- **Classify the AI's role on three axes: critical or complementary, reactive or proactive, and degree of autonomy.** — Her system is complementary (claims are already reviewed without AI), reactive (triggered by claim submission), and at most semi-autonomous because of the human-review rules. ([6:44](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=404))
- **Choose the data retrieval method per source, based on the kind of content and how often it changes.** — Guidelines and policies are long PDFs. Chunk them, embed the chunks, extract metadata, and use vector search with metadata pre-filters or hybrid search so codes are matched exactly. Claims history is well-structured, so an exact match on patient ID is enough, after removing PII. Update cadence is annual for guidelines, quarterly for policies, and hourly for claims, which is tied to the one-hour target. ([9:07](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=547))
- **Start with the simplest architecture, evaluate it, and add complexity only where the evals show gaps. Don't start with an agent.** — She maps the claim flow first, then picks patterns. The pattern is RAG for retrieval, a controlled workflow with a human-in-the-loop step, and an LLM-as-router considered but not chosen for now. Fine-tuning is only for behavioral failures, not data or orchestration failures. ([11:35](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=695))
- **Define guardrails on both inputs and outputs, and turn each one into a metric you track.** — Input guardrails reject irrelevant or harmful requests (for example, a poem request on a claims system). The output guardrail requires a citation for every approve or deny decision. Track the input rejection rate and the missing-citation rate, and measure faithfulness to the retrieved sources. ([21:07](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=1267))
- **After launch, track the offline metrics plus implicit signals from real use. The human override rate is the main one, and review time is the second.** — A rising override rate means the AI verdicts are wrong. Long human review times can point to verbose or confusing AI output. Both need a threshold and an investigation trigger. ([24:45](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=1485))
- **Optimize the information in the context window for accuracy, and pick cost and latency tactics such as caching and batching only once the accuracy is good.** — Accuracy levers: prompt engineering, reranking retrieved chunks, and persisting cross-session memory. Cost and latency levers: semantic caching of similar past cases and batching. Reliability: use structured outputs so each response always includes the decision and the citations. ([25:46](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=1546))

## Techniques
- **Four-phase AI system design framework** — Work in order: write the business problem and SMART success metrics, list constraints, then design data, architecture, and UX, then define evals and monitoring, then optimize for cost, latency, and reliability. *When:* Before building any AI product that other people depend on, especially in regulated domains, and before handing the design to a coding agent.
- **Quantified solution-agnostic business problem** — State the user, the current manual process, a measured baseline, and the gap versus an industry standard. Leave out the system design. *When:* At the start of a project, to set a measurable target and avoid deciding the architecture too early.
- **Three-axis AI role classification** — Rate the AI's role as critical or complementary, reactive or proactive, and of low to high autonomy. Let the autonomy level come from the business's human-review rules. *When:* When scoping how much to automate, and when stakeholders need a clear decision on how much the AI can do alone.
- **Per-source retrieval strategy from update cadence and content type** — For each data source, decide on chunking, embedding, metadata extraction, and PII removal. Choose vector search with pre-filters or hybrid search for long text with codes, and exact keyed lookup for structured records. Set the pipeline cadence from how often the source changes. *When:* When the system pulls from several sources with different formats and update rates, and stale data would be costly.
- **Simplest-design-first, evaluate, then iterate** — Map the end-to-end flow of one real case. Start with the simplest pattern that fits (RAG plus control flow plus human-in-the-loop). Add agents, routers, or fine-tuning only when the eval results show a gap that calls for them. *When:* When there is pressure to build an agent by default, or when a coding agent is proposing the architecture.
- **Guardrails as measurable rates** — Define what counts as invalid on input and output. Track each as a rate: input rejection rate, missing-citation rate, and faithfulness of the decision to the retrieved text. *When:* For any LLM output that has consequences and needs citations or policy checks.
- **Production signals from human behaviour** — Track the rate at which human reviewers override the AI, and how long they take to review each recommendation. Alert when either crosses a threshold, and investigate. *When:* After launch, when real labels are rare and reviewer actions are the best available quality signal.
- **Structured outputs as a reliability guard** — Force the model to return a schema that includes the decision and the citations, so downstream code can check that each response is complete. *When:* When every answer must carry evidence, and missing fields would break the workflow or the audit.

## Numbers
- Medical reviewers take an average of 2 days per claim, which is 4x the industry standard for non-urgent cases and 12x for urgent ones. This is the illustrative baseline in the example. ([3:49](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=229))
- Success target: cut average urgent-claim processing time from 2 days to 1 hour within 90 days of launch. ([7:49](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=469))
- Update cadence for the example data: clinical guidelines annually, coverage policies quarterly, claims history hourly. ([10:20](https://youtu.be/T0HhO4YtTfE?t=620))

## Hot takes
- Vibe coding is dangerous once other people depend on the output, and the product spec is now the hard part rather than the code.
- Starting with an agent, or letting a coding agent choose the architecture, usually over-engineers the system.
