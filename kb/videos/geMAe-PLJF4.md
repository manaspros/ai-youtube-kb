> Nick Nisi (WorkOS) demos a fruit-fly connectome trained to approve or reject code diffs, and argues humans should review for intent and proof of work, not bugs.

## Summary
Nick Nisi argues that the bottleneck in software delivery is now human PR review, since agents and CI already catch most defects. He says human reviewers should shift from finding bugs to understanding why a change is being made and what it does. The centerpiece is a playful demo: a simulated male fruit fly connectome, trained on good and bad code, that approves or rejects diffs via an LLM-generated "smell" input. In a live audience test the room got 3 of 3 and the fly 2 of 3, and he reports roughly 88% accuracy in his own testing, with 96% and 98% on harder sets. He is candid that the fly cannot explain its verdicts or suggest fixes. He closes by proposing a tiered review pipeline (team-owned checklist skill, lightweight classifier, automated reviewers, human escalation only on failure) plus proof-of-work artifacts such as screenshots, test output and smoke tests. The talk ends with a brief WorkOS booth plug.

## Key ideas
- **The bottleneck is now human PR review: code is written and PRs are opened faster than ever, and CI and agent reviewers are waiting on a person.** — Speaker says the pipeline is stuck waiting on him, so he has become the bottleneck. ([0:12](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=12))
- **Human review should shift from catching bugs to bringing context: what changes and why it matters to the reviewer.** — Automated reviewers (Greptile, CodeRabbit and similar) are trusted for defect detection; the human's job becomes intent and business context. He says humans will keep a hand in because only they can explain why functionality changes. ([1:10](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=70))
- **The fruit-fly demo: a connectome of a male fruit fly brain (about 176,000 neurons) is wired into a pass/fail reviewer.** — He says a teacher reinforces the fly's responses with sugar-water (good code) and bitter (bad code) signals, and the verdict is read from whether the fly's tongue extends. He reports a memory subset of neurons was the only part he changed. ([4:05](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=245))
- **An LLM converts the diff into a sensory input: about 30 non-biased questions about the code, passed to the fly as a smell.** — He describes the LLM as the fly's nose; the brain's learned memories do the judging. He says removing those memories makes the fly behave randomly, which he uses as evidence the brain is doing the processing rather than the LLM. ([5:26](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=326))
- **Live audience test: room 3 of 3 correct, fly 2 of 3, with his own testing showing about 88% accuracy.** — Three random files from a real codebase were shown; the fly was wrong on the first, matched the room on the second, and matched on the third. He reports 96% correct on a first, harder test set and 98% on a plausible-mistake set. ([8:55](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=535))
- **The fly can spot changed or removed conditionals and simple logic regressions, but it cannot say why or how to fix them.** — He says it only reports a smell (roughly, "I don't like this"), so the humans still need to explain intent. Dedicated reviewers such as Greptile or CodeRabbit do the job much better. ([11:36](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=696))
- **Proposed pipeline: a team-owned review skill, then a lightweight classifier, then automated reviewers, then human escalation only on failure.** — Team skill runs on PRs tagged to the team via code owners and encodes the team's minimum review checklist. The classifier checks complexity, whether the change matches its description, and risk. Any failure escalates to a human; clean passes merge without one. ([12:37](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=757))
- **When humans stop reading the code, verification should be proven by the agent with artifacts, not by reading.** — He suggests screenshots, videos, test results, command outputs, smoke tests, or easy replication steps for manual checks. He expects human review to shrink as trust in these methods grows, and that each team (DX vs platform) must define what counts as human-worthy. ([15:02](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=902))
- **After a failure, audit the pipeline and improve the team skill or automated reviewer, then have AI fix the change and summarize the rationale and discarded alternatives back to the human.** — This is the feedback loop: each escape becomes a rule update in the review skill or reviewer config. ([14:00](https://youtu.be/geMAe-PLJF4?t=840))

## Techniques
- **Team-owned review skill as a gate** — Keep a versioned skill or script per team that encodes the minimum checklist that team's reviewers apply. Trigger it on PRs that touch code-owner-tagged paths. Pass the PR forward only if the checklist passes; otherwise escalate. Update the skill after each escape. *When:* Multi-team codebases where different owners have different review standards (for example, DX vs platform teams with YAML).
- **Tiered PR escalation pipeline** — Stage 1: team review skill. Stage 2: lightweight classifier (LLM or small model) scoring complexity, whether the code does what the PR says, and risk. Stage 3: automated reviewer bot (for example Greptile or CodeRabbit). Escalate to a human on any failure; auto-merge when all stages pass. Post an audit summary on any break. *When:* High PR volume where human review is the bottleneck and most changes are low risk.
- **Agent proof-of-work bundle for review** — Require the implementing agent to attach verification evidence to each PR: screenshots, video recordings, test output, command output, smoke tests, and steps to reproduce manually. A reviewer then checks the evidence rather than reading all code. *When:* When reviewers no longer read every line and need to confirm that behavior matches intent.
- **Measure the reviewer on a labelled diff set** — He evaluated his system on real diffs with known approve or reject verdicts, reporting a hit rate on a standard set (about 88%) and on harder adversarial or plausible-mistake sets (96% and 98% on specific cases). Measure separately for easy and plausible-mistake cases, and report sample sizes. *When:* Before trusting any automated reviewer to block or auto-merge PRs.
- **LLM as structured feature extractor before a non-LLM classifier** — Use an LLM to answer a fixed set of neutral questions (he used about 30) about a diff, producing a feature vector. Feed that vector to a separate, trained decision model. He reports that the decision model, not the LLM, performs the judgment. *When:* When you want a cheap, consistent, auditable decision layer on top of LLM-derived signals. Note: no implementation details were given; the approach is a speaker demo.

## Hot takes
- The fruit fly is a gimmick: the accuracy numbers (88%, 96%, 98%) come from unpublished internal tests with no methodology, sample size or false-positive rate stated.
- The live audience test is three files. Its outcome (room 3 of 3, fly 2 of 3) is anecdote, not evidence.
- The claim that the fly brain, not the LLM, does the judging rests on one memory-removal observation.
- Speaker's stance that humans should stop looking for bugs ('only a human could find it... I don't believe that that's the case anymore') is an opinion, not a finding.
